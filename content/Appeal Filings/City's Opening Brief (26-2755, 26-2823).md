INTRODUCTION
Homelessness is “a complex and difficult human issue.” City of Grants Pass, 
Or. v. Johnson, 603 U.S. 520, 535 (2024). Addressing it requires “the full panoply 
of tools in the policy toolbox” to balance helping unhoused residents with
protecting community health and safety. Id. For this reason, the people and their 
elected representatives, not federal judges, are best positioned to devise an 
effective response. Id.
The district court here, however, has repeatedly substituted its own judgment 
for the people’s. For more than year, it blocked a critical health and safety 
encampment abatement despite a deadly Leptospirosis outbreak. In doing so, it 
routinely disregarded basic civil procedure principles and binding precedent. 
First, in orders already on appeal, the court granted Berkeley Homeless 
Union (“BHU”) what became a nine-month preliminary injunction on a ground—
the City’s supposed failure to “complete” the Americans with Disabilities Act
(“ADA”) interactive process—that the court later recognized had no legal basis.
[[ER 1863-1865 - Civil Minutes#ER-1864|10-ER-1864]]. With little analysis, the court also erroneously found that BHU,
which purports to be an association but has never produced evidence proving it, 
had standing to pursue ADA reasonable accommodations claims related to the
abatement for an unknown number of unhoused individuals with different 
disabilities and needs. [[ER 1863-1865 - Civil Minutes#ER-1863|10-ER-1863]]-65.

Nine months later, in the summary judgment and related rulings on appeal 
here, the court imported its preliminary standing analysis without revisiting it or 
making findings based on admissible evidence. On equally dubious grounds, the 
court partially granted summary judgment for BHU on certain ADA, Fourth 
Amendment and Fourteenth Amendment state-created danger claims, and—in a 
move again bending basic rules of civil procedure—simultaneously granted on the 
same grounds (1) a “preliminary” injunction to 16 new BHU members who first 
moved after summary judgment briefing and fact discovery had completed; and (2)
a preliminary injunction to a putative class of plaintiffs in a different lawsuit. 1-

Aside from serious procedural flaws, the substance of these decisions runs
roughshod over binding precedent. On standing, the court effectively allowed BHU 
to circumvent Rule 23, ignoring black letter law prohibiting associations from 
asserting fact-specific claims that require individualized proof on behalf of their 
members. See NetChoice, LLC v. Bonta, 152 F.4th 1002, 1014 (9th Cir. 2025). On 
the Fourth Amendment, the court disregarded a long line of community caretaking 
cases establishing that cities may impound vehicles, including those used as 
shelter, for valid health and safety reasons such as the Leptospirosis outbreak here. 
See, e.g., S. Dakota v. Opperman, 428 U.S. 364, 368-69 (1976). On state-created 
danger, the court stretched precedent beyond recognition, extending a narrow

doctrine to hold the City legally responsible for inherent difficulties associated 
with homelessness it did not cause. See 3PAK LLC v. City of Seattle, 174 F.4th

b. The City Undertook an Extensive Interactive Process While Risks 
from a Deadly Disease Mounted.
Following the notice, the City received numerous requests for ADA

SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT
First, the district court erred in finding BHU had associational standing for 
ADA reasonable accommodations and state-danger claims. Such claims are too 
fact-specific to support associational standing. See, e.g., NetChoice, LLC, 152 
F.4th at 1014. Although the court ignored this third element of the associational 
standing test, it could not do so, as the requirement is not optional. BHU also lacks 
standing for all its claims for other reasons, including that it failed to provide 
admissible evidence, and that associations lack standing to assert Fourth 
Amendment claims vicariously. See Knife Rights, Inc. v. Vance, 802 F.3d 377, 387 
(2d Cir. 2015).
Second, the court erred in ruling in Plaintiffs’ favor on their Fourth 
Amendment claims challenging the possible impoundment, despite ample notice, 
of vehicles used as shelter. Binding precedent, which the court ignored, permits
cities to impound vehicles, even those used as shelter, for valid public health and 
safety reasons like the ones here. See, e.g., Opperman, 428 U.S. at 368-69.
Third, the court erred in ruling for Plaintiffs on the state-created danger 
doctrine. The doctrine only applies when the state causes serious bodily harm such 
Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (32 of 74)

as assault, sexual abuse, or death, which the plaintiff would not have faced 
otherwise. 3PAK LLC, 174 F.4th at 1184-85. The only harms from abatement are 
inherent to homelessness, which the City did not cause. And even if the abatement 
were to slightly worsen Plaintiffs’ situation, any resulting harm does not rise to the 
high level required to support a claim. Id. Plaintiffs also cannot show that the City 
acted with deliberate indifference because the City provided ample notice and 
alternatives.
Fourth, the court erred in granting certain ADA accommodations. Its chief 
error rests in defining the City’s ADA “program” to include allowing unhoused 
people to camp in tents or vehicles on City streets. In fact, the City does not allow

II. The District Court Erred in Finding That BHU Has Standing.
a. BHU Lacks Standing for ADA and State-Created Danger Claims 
Because They Require Individualized Proof.
Organizations like BHU must establish associational standing or 
organizational standing. Satanic Temple v. Labrador, 149 F.4th 1047, 1051-54 (9th 
Cir. 2025). Only associational standing is at issue here. [[ER 1802-1862 - Transcript of Proceedings June 10, 2025#ER-1806|10-ER-1806]]:19-22. BHU 
would have associational standing only if it could establish that (1) its members 
would have standing to sue in their own right; (2) the interests it seeks to protect 
are germane to its purpose; and (3) neither the claim asserted nor the relief 
requested requires the participation of individual members in the lawsuit.

IV. The District Court’s State-Created Danger Rulings Relating to the 9-
Square-Foot Rule and Vehicles Are Erroneous.

obligation” on the state to protect life, liberty or property, the state may be liable if 
it creates or exposes “individuals to danger they otherwise would not have faced.”

“always been reluctant to expand” to “untrodden ground.” Id.

recognition. Every state-created danger case in this Court has involved conduct 
causing serious and immediate bodily harm such as assault, sexual abuse, or death.

removal, upon giving ample notice, of potentially-contaminated tents and vehicles 
used as shelter, based on harms of homelessness caused to people who already lack

A plaintiff can succeed on a Title II ADA claim only by showing they “were 
either excluded from participation in or denied the benefits of a public entity’s 
services, programs, or activities, or w[ere] otherwise discriminated against by the 
public entity.” Caltrans, 32 F.4th at 859-60 (citing 42 U.S.C. § 12132). While a 
plaintiff can show the second element if a city fails to make “reasonable 
modifications in policies, practices, or procedures when the modifications are 
necessary to avoid discrimination on the basis of disability,” a city can defeat that 
claim by “demonstrat[ing] that making the modifications would fundamentally 
alter the nature of the service, program or activity.” Id. 
Before discussing the court’s specific accommodation rulings, the City first 
addresses the district court’s improper “program” analysis, from which all its 
errors flow. If left uncorrected, the district court’s analysis will, paradoxically, 
disincentivize cities from offering services to their unhoused populations.
Under this Court’s precedent, Title II applies not just to formal public 
programs but to “anything a public entity does.” Cohen v. City of Culver City, 754 
F.3d 690, 695 (9th Cir. 2014). But that does not mean that a particular Title II 
“program” encompasses everything a public entity does. It also does not mean, as 
the district court believed, that courts must conduct a complex “program” analysis 
focusing on “the range of services traditionally offered by the public entity” or the 
Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (57 of 74)

The correct analysis is much simpler. In a Title II case seeking to modify a 
generally applicable law or enforcement action, the “program” is the law or action 
the plaintiff seeks to modify. Courts then ask whether the proposed modification
would fundamentally alter that “program’s” purpose. If a disabled plaintiff seeks to 
modify a zoning ordinance, for instance, the “program” is the zoning ordinance, 
and courts assess whether the modification would fundamentally alter the “basic 
purpose of zoning [ ] to bring complementary land uses together, while separating 
incompatible ones.” Ohio House, LLC v. City of Costa Mesa, 135 F.4th 645, 678 
(9th Cir. 2025). If a plaintiff seeks to modify a dog quarantine law, the “program” 
is the law, and courts assess whether the modification would alter the law’s
purpose of preventing disease. Crowder v. Kitagawa, 81 F.3d 1480, 1485-86 (9th 
Cir. 1996). And most pertinently, if a plaintiff seeks to modify procedures for 
abating an encampment, the “program” is the abatement policies, and courts assess 
whether the requested modification would fundamentally alter the abatement’s
purpose. Caltrans, 32 F.4th at 862.

accommodation’s impact for one or a “handful” of individuals. The Supreme Court 
has explained that “fundamental alterations” occur “in two different ways.” PGA 
Tour, Inc. v. Martin, 532 U.S. 661, 682-83 (2001). First, if a modification request 
alters “an essential aspect” of the program, it is a fundamental alteration as a matter
