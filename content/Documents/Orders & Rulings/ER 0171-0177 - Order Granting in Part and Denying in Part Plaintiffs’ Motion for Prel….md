I. INTRODUCTION & BACKGROUND
This matter arises out of the City of Berkeley’s planned abatement of the encampment 
located along 8th Street and Harrison Street (the “Harrison Encampment” or “Encampment”). 
On March 5, 2026, Plaintiffs Yesica Prado, Erin Spencer, Amber Whitson, Jermaine 
White, and Monique Williams, and organizational plaintiff Where Do We Go Berkeley, filed a 
motion for a temporary restraining order (“TRO”). [[ER 1040-1062 - Plaintiff’s Notice of Motion and Motion for TRO and Order to Show Caus…|Dkt. 178]]. The Court issued a TRO on March 
7, 2026, granting protection with respect to vehicle seizures and denying Plaintiffs’ remaining 
requests.1 [[ER 0833-0837 - Order Granting in Part and Denying in Part Motion for TRO|Dkt. 187]]. The Court ruled that the City could not seize, impound, or destroy any 
vehicles in the planned abatement zone “absent a specific, fact-based determination that the 
particular vehicle obstructs traffic or poses a concrete public safety threat.” Id.

II. DISCUSSION
A party seeking a preliminary injunction must show “that he is likely to succeed on the 
merits, that he is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief, that the 
balance of equities tips in his favor, and that an injunction is in the public interest.”  Winter v. 
NRDC, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008).
For the reasons previously stated, the Court finds Plaintiffs have established a likelihood of 
irreparable harm absent the relief granted herein. As to the balance of equities and public interest, 
those factors lie in relative equipoise. Plaintiffs have established that the loss of shelter is a 
significant risk that would expose them to the elements and threaten their physical safety. On the 
other hand, the City has made a substantial showing that the leptospirosis outbreak poses a serious 
and growing risk of harm to Encampment residents and the surrounding community. Accordingly, 
Plaintiffs’ showing on the likelihood of success on the merits is decisive here. See Meinecke v. 
City of Seattle, 99 F.4th 514, 521 (9th Cir. 2024) (likelihood of success on the merits “is the most 
important factor in the preliminary injunction analysis”). 
A. Safe Relocation Site
First, Plaintiffs request an order requiring the City to designate a safe relocation site 
outside the abatement zone. The Court’s TRO order denied this relief, and the Court affirms that 
denial. [[ER 0833-0837 - Order Granting in Part and Denying in Part Motion for TRO|Dkt. 187]]. The Court’s order on the parties’ cross-motions for summary judgment in the 
related Berkeley Homeless Union v. City of Berkeley (“BHU”) case concludes that such an 
accommodation would be both an unreasonable accommodation and a fundamental alteration of 
the City’s program. No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, Dkt. 288. That reasoning applies with equal force 
here. Accordingly, Plaintiffs have not demonstrated a likelihood of success on the merits as to this 
form of relief, and the motion for preliminary injunction is DENIED IN PART with respect to the

B. Replacement of Property
Second, Plaintiffs ask for an order requiring the City to furnish replacement survival gear 
— including tents, sleeping gear, and clothing — that it seizes and destroys as part of the 
abatement. The Court incorporates by reference its analysis of this claim in its order on the 
parties’ cross-motions for summary judgment in the BHU case. No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, Dkt. 288. 
For residents who have not submitted ADA accommodation requests, the City’s advance notice of 
over one month satisfies the constitutional minimum. See Shipp v. Schaaf, 379 F. Supp. 3d 1033 
(N.D. Cal. 2019); Sullivan v. City of Berkeley, 383 F. Supp. 3d 976 (N.D. Cal. 2019). The Court 
reasserts, however, that the City’s storage obligations are not wholly eliminated by the 
leptospirosis outbreak. As the BHU summary judgment order explained, the City may decline to 
handle or store items that are observably wet, muddy, and plausibly contaminated with 
leptospirosis, but generalized assertions about leptospirosis risks do not satisfy the City’s burden 
of demonstrating that any storage is categorically unsafe or infeasible. No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, 
Dkt. 288. The City must act in compliance with the BHU summary judgment order, which 
explains constitutional minimum standards governing the seizure and destruction of property 
during Encampment abatement operations. The City has not demonstrated that property such as 
medical equipment and mobility devices cannot be safely handled and stored, particularly given 
the availability of, e.g., PPE and plastic bags.
Relatedly, the Court’s summary judgment order in BHU explained that the destruction of 
tents may trigger the state-created danger doctrine. No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, Dkt. 288. 
Accordingly, as a constitutional minimum, the City may not destroy tents or primary shelters of 
residents unless it is voluntarily abandoned or the City makes a specific, documented finding that 
the tent or shelter item poses a threat to public health or safety that cannot be addressed through 
bagging, containment or other less intrusive handling measures. To the extent that the City’s 
abatement, including enforcement of rules governing the occupation of space on sidewalks, would 
result in the destruction of tents or shelters through no fault of the unhoused resident, the City is 
obligated to allow the unhoused individual to retain their tent, consider reasonable

C. Vehicle Seizures
Finally, the Court addresses the vehicle seizure claim. The constitutional framework for 
vehicle seizures was explained in this Court’s prior rulings and in the BHU summary judgment 
order. Dkt. 87; No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, Dkt. 288. The impoundment of RVs implicates the 
Fourth Amendment’s protection against unreasonable seizures, see Dkt. 87 at 26 (“The City has 
the burden to establish that the taking was warranted on a fact-specific basis.”) (citing U.S. v. 
Cervantes, 703 F.3d 1135, 1144 (9th Cir. 2012)), and the state-created danger doctrine under the 
Due Process Clause. See id. at 50 (“When Plaintiffs’ shelter and personal belongings are 
destroyed, and they have no safe shelter alternative nor means to protect themselves from the 
elements, the state has made plaintiffs’ conditions worse.”); Janosko v. City of Oakland, 2023 WL 
187499, at *3 (N.D. Cal. Jan. 13, 2023) (holding that removal of shelter “affirmatively expose[s] 
[unhoused plaintiffs] to harsher, more dangerous conditions”); Alfred v. City of Vallejo, 2025 WL 
435900, at *9 (E.D. Cal. Feb. 7, 2025).
As noted in the BHU summary judgement order, the City may invoke the community 
caretaker doctrine to seize vehicles, but only upon a specific, individualized showing that the 
particular vehicle obstructs traffic or presents a concrete, documented threat to public health and 
safety. No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, Dkt. 288. Here the City has presented evidence of a potential 
health threat posed by the vehicles. [[ER 0615-0618 - Declaration of Peter Radu re Inspection of Vehicles Pursuant to Court’…|Dkt. 193]]. But as explained in the BHU summary judgment 
order, it has not made an individualized showing of the magnitude of the threat posed by each 
vehicle. No. 25-cv-01414-EMC, Dkt. 288. More importantly, even if a potential threat to public 
health is demonstrated, before seizing a vehicle used as shelter, the City must attempt less 
restrictive alternatives, including targeted pest treatment, nuisance abatement orders, debris 
removal, sealing of open engine compartments, and assisted voluntary relocation — or it must 
demonstrate with particularity why such alternatives are infeasible due to that specific vehicle and

Furthermore, the affirmative act of seizing and destroying a vehicle that serves as shelter 
implicates the state-created danger doctrine, as such a seizure would transform an unhoused but 
sheltered person into one who is unhoused and unsheltered. Id. The City must therefore undergo 
a graduated framework similar to that applied under the Fourth Amendment and procedural due 
process. Id.
Where a vehicle is used for storage only and not as primary shelter, the constitutional harm 
is less acute, and the protection against such seizure rests on the Fourth Amendment, not the statecreated danger doctrine (which applies to shelter/housing). Under the Fourth Amendment, the 
requirement of a specific, individualized fact-based showing of a concrete public safety threat, and 
the opportunity for remediation, remain in effect.
As explained in the Court’s BHU summary judgment order, id., before proceeding to 
forcibly impound or destroy vehicles without consent, constitutional standards mandate that the 
City: (1) make a specific, fact-based determination — beyond mere parking violations, 
inoperability, or expired registration — that the particular vehicle obstructs traffic or poses a 
concrete, individualized public health or safety threat; (2) provide the vehicle’s owner with written 
notice of the specific findings, an opportunity to respond, and a reasonable opportunity to 
remediate the identified condition or voluntarily relocate the vehicle; and (3) pursue less restrictive 
alternatives, including targeted cleaning, pest treatment, containment orders, and assisted 
relocation, or demonstrate with particularity why such alternatives are ineffective for the given 
vehicle and occupant. Id. Only upon satisfaction of all three requirements may the City proceed 
to more drastic measures such as impoundment. Moreover, prior to the destruction of any vehicle, 
the City must further demonstrate that no alternative remedy can adequately mitigate the health or 
safety risk presented by the vehicle. Id.
Accordingly, the Court finds a likelihood of success on the merits with respect to the 
vehicle-related constitutional claims, and the motion for preliminary injunction is GRANTED IN 
PART with respect to such relief.

III. CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs’ motion for preliminary injunction is GRANTED IN 
PART AND DENIED IN PART. 
Pending final adjudication of this case, the City is hereby enjoined as follows:
1. The City shall assist with physical relocation or storage of property unless that property 
is plausibly contaminated with leptospirosis or presents a genuine health or safety risk. The City 
must consider methods of safely handling and storing items that do not present an obvious risk to 
health and safety. 
2. The City is prohibited from summarily destroying property necessary for survival (i.e.,
tents, sleeping equipment, and clothing), if it cannot be moved by the individual, without 
providing a replacement therefor. To the extent abatement and enforcement of sidewalk space 
limitations would result in the destruction of tents, the City must either: (1) allow the unhoused 
individual to retain their tent if it can be relocated to an area in which does not unreasonably 
obstruct sidewalk passage (e.g. allowing for six-foot corridor), or (2) provide a replacement tent 
that will accommodate the City’s maintenance of reasonable passage (e.g. six feet) on sidewalks. 
3. The City is prohibited from towing, impounding, or destroying vehicles without first (1) 
making an individualized determination that the specific vehicle obstructs traffic or presents an 
actual health or safety threat, (2) providing the vehicle owner with notice and a reasonable 
opportunity to abate said conditions, and (3) pursuing less restrictive alternatives to impoundment 
or destruction. Prior to the destruction of any vehicle, the City must further demonstrate that no 
alternative remedy can adequately mitigate the health or safety risk presented by the vehicle.
In all other respects, the Plaintiff’s motion for preliminary injunction is denied.
