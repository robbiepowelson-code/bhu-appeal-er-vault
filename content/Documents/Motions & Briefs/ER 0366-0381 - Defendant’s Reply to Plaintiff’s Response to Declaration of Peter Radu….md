I. INTRODUCTION 
On March 7, 2026, this Court issued its Order granting in part and denying in part Plaintiffs’ 
Motion for Temporary Restraining Order (the “TRO Order”). ([[ER 0833-0837 - Order Granting in Part and Denying in Part Motion for TRO|ECF No. 187]].) In granting the TRO 
with respect to vehicle seizure, the Court’s Order stated the following: 
the City has the burden to prove that the vehicles they impounded were ‘impeding 
traffic or threatening public safety and convenience.’”). The City has not met that 
requirement. The City has merely offered a general assertion that stationary vehicles 
may harbor rodent nests or impede abatement efforts. Opposition to TRO, Ex. 1 
(Peter Radu Declaration) at ¶¶ 18–21. There have been no individualized 
inspections or evaluations of any vehicles in this regard. Moreover, the City’s 
claim that it is impossible to search for signs of rat infestation under the vehicles 
makes little sense, especially considering the city could assist residents in moving 
the vehicle several feet to obtain access to the ground. As this Court previously held, 
a vehicle posing no specific, documented threat to public safety or traffic may not be 
seized under the community caretaker doctrine. Order Granting in Part and Denying 
in Part Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss (Dkt 51) at 23–27. 
The TRO is therefore GRANTED with respect to vehicle seizure. The City may not 
seize, impound or destroy any vehicle in the planned abatement zone absent a 
specific, fact-based determination that the particular vehicle obstructs traffic or 
poses a concrete public safety threat. 
([[ER 0833-0837 - Order Granting in Part and Denying in Part Motion for TRO|ECF No.187]] a p. 4:12-26 (emphasis added).) 
On March 10, 2026, Defendant City of Berkeley (the “City”) provided the Court with the 
specific, fact-based determination that the particular vehicles in question obstruct traffic or pose a 
concrete public safety threat as required by the TRO Order. Specifically, the City filed the 
Declaration of Peter Radu Re: Inspection of Vehicles Pursuant to Court’s March 7, 2026 Order (the 
“Radu Declaration”). ([[ER 0615-0618 - Declaration of Peter Radu re Inspection of Vehicles Pursuant to Court’…|ECF No. 193]].) In the Radu Declaration, the City Manager explained that, on 
March 7, 2026, the Berkeley Environmental Health Division and Berkeley Fire Department 
conducted individualized inspections and evaluations of the vehicles in question in accordance with 
the TRO Order. The Radu Declaration also attached the inspection records of both agencies. (Radu 
Declaration ¶ 4, 5, Exhs. A, B.) These inspections provided the Court with specific, fact-based 
determinations that the vehicles in question obstruct traffic and/or pose a concrete public safety 
threat.

II. ARGUMENT 
As explained above in the Introduction, the Court’s TRO Order required the City to perform 
individual inspections of the vehicles and provide the Court with specific, fact-based determinations 
that the vehicles obstruct traffic and/or pose a concrete public safety threat. According to the Court’s 
Order Re Peter Radu Declaration, the City has now done that. Nonetheless, Plaintiffs’ Response 
focuses on an incorrect legal standard for vehicle seizure. The Court should ignore these arguments 
because the Radu Declaration provides “particularized evidence of threats to public safety from 
certain vehicles in the area” and meets the required of the Court’s Order. Moreover, the applicable 
legal standards clearly allow the City to enforce Vehicle Code requirements even absent such a 
particularized showing. The fact that Plaintiffs are seeking to move the bar even further is a gross 
departure from established case law and barely meets the standard for good faith, nonfrivolous 
advocacy. The Court should reject Plaintiffs’ specious argument and allow the City to apply ordinary 
and unremarkable standards for the regulation of public streets. 
A. The Radu Declaration Shows A Concrete Public Safety Threat

phrase: res ipsa loquitur. The only conclusion that can be drawn from the Radu Declaration and, 
more specifically, the inspection records from the Berkeley Environmental Health Division and 
Berkeley Fire Department is that the vehicles in question pose a health and safety threat to the public 
and to the vehicle owners themselves.

429 F.3d 858, 864–66 (9th Cir. 2005).) The City must show that, for the purposes of the Fourth 
Amendment, the vehicles were “impeding traffic or threatening public safety and convenience,” not 
merely that the seizure was authorized by state law. (Miranda, 429 F.3d at 864–65.) That inquiry

Vehicles documented by health and fire inspectors to have rodent droppings in their vicinity and

the kind of concrete, record-based showing the Fourth Amendment requires. (See id. at 1141–42.)

factual basis for a caretaking seizure; here, the City has produced sworn declarations, inspection 
records, and expert public-health evidence identifying the vehicles as part of the hazard and their 
removal as part of the mitigation. 
Nor does Long v. Gill assist Plaintiffs. There, the court denied summary judgment because 
the vehicle was parked on private property with permission, was neither impeding traffic nor 
creating a hazard, and the record did not conclusively establish that impoundment was reasonably 
necessary to protect it from theft or vandalism. (Long v. Gill, 981 F. Supp. 2d 966, 970–71 (D. Or. 
2013).) Here, by contrast, the City has submitted evidence aimed directly at present health and fire 
risks associated with leaving these vehicles in place on public streets in the middle of an active 
disease outbreak. Again, the evidence speaks for itself.

It also bears emphasis that the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment protections recognized in 
Lavan v. City of Los Angeles, 693 F.3d 1022 (9th Cir. 2012), do not foreclose the City’s action. The 
Fourth Amendment protects against unreasonable interference with possessory interests, regardless 
of whether there is an invasion of privacy. (Miranda, 429 F.3d at 862.) And homeless persons 
retain protected possessory interests in unabandoned property. (Lavan, 693 F.3d at 1028–29.) But 
those interests do not bar seizure where there is an immediate threat to public health or safety. Lavan
itself described an injunction that permitted the City to seize property that “presents an immediate 
threat to public health or safety.” (Id. at 1026.) That is precisely what the record establishes here. 
In short, the City is not relying on a statute alone, a policy alone, or a bare assertion of 
danger. It is relying on sworn declarations, health and fire inspection records, and public-health

Plaintiffs do not dispute that the four vehicles the City intends to remove are either 
inoperable, unregistered, or both, and as such there is no Fourth Amendment violation associated 
with their lawful towing. While the Fourth Amendment protects people and their effects “against 
unreasonable searches and seizures,” there are recognized exceptions for warrantless tows. (See 
Cady v. Dombrowski, 413 U.S. 433 (1973).) In particular, police officers are justified in impounding 
vehicles that “jeopardize [] the public safety.” (Opperman, 428 U.S. at 369.) An impoundment may 
be proper if its condition prevents the driver from lawfully operating the vehicle or if necessary to 
remove the vehicle from an exposed or public location. (Miranda, 429 F.3d at 865.) Importantly, 
the Supreme Court has made clear that “[t]he authority of police to seize and remove from the streets 
vehicles impeding traffic or threatening public safety and convenience is beyond challenge.” (Id. 
(emphasis added).) 
Pursuant to the City’s abatement notice and no parking signs, the City intends to tow four 
vehicles—an RV, a sedan, a station wagon, and a minivan. (Radu Declaration ¶ 6.) Three of those 
vehicles have expired registrations (the sedan, station wagon, and minivan) and three are inoperable 
(the RV, sedan, and station wagon). (Id.) 
Beginning with the unregistered vehicles, the California Vehicle code provides that “[i]f a 
vehicle is found or operated upon a highway, public land... [w]ith a registration expiration date in 
excess of six months before the date it is found” an officer may remove the vehicle. (Cal. Veh. 
Code § 22651(o)(A)(i).) Plaintiffs do not contest that the station wagon and minivan’s registrations 
are both more than six months out of date. (ECF No. 199.) Courts have consistently found that a 
warrantless towing of a car without a valid registration is permitted because leaving it would 
otherwise create a risk that the car would be driven while unregistered, which is also a violation of 
the California Vehicle Code.1 (See, e.g., Halajian v. D & B Towing, 209 Cal. App. 4th 1, 16 (2012) 
(finding no Fourth Amendment violation when car with expired registration was seized); Crago v.
