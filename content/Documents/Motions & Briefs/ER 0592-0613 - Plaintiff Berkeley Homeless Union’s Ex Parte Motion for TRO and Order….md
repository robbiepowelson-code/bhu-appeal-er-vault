 INTRODUCTION
This ex parte application for TRO seeks to enjoin the City of Berkeley from clearing out 
BHU members on March 10th after the denials. This court specifically noted the viability of 
residents members ADA claims in its March 7th granting in part and denying in part the 
temporary restraining order in Prado v. City of Berkeley et al, 23-cv-04537-EMC, ECF 187. 
“The City represented that approximately fifteen encampment residents submitted ADA 
accommodations requests” in connection with the planned abatement. In addition to the 7 or 8 
residents who are exempt from the abatement notice. The City represented on the record that it is 
engaged in the interactive process with those individuals and will not forcibly remove any of 
them until that process is complete. The Court relies upon the City’s representation. This Order 
is without prejudice to considering any future motion for relief based on ADA Claims.

To gather the information necessary to submit accommodation requests, Prado and BHU 
Officers personally meet with encampment residents to discuss their disabilities and the 
accommodations they require. These conversations typically occur at residents’ campsites and 
often last between thirty minutes and one hour. During these meetings, residents are asked about 
their disabilities, how long they have experienced them, how their disabilities affect daily 
activities, what tasks are difficult or impossible for them to perform, and what assistance is 
necessary to prevent the City’s enforcement actions from aggravating their conditions. Prado and 
BHU Officers then document the residents’ responses on BHU accommodation request forms, 
which residents review and sign to confirm their accuracy and authorize the release of their 
medical information for verification by healthcare providers. 
Using this information, BHU submits ADA accommodation requests to the City that 
include a description of each individual’s disability, the nexus between the disability and the 
requested accommodation, and the specific accommodations sought. BHU also works with 
medical providers, including the Lifelong Medical Street Team, to obtain medical verification 
letters of the residents’ disabilities, as these letters are requested by the city of Berkeley to 
proceed with the interactive process.
Prado has been the primary BHU representative communicating with the City regarding 
ADA accommodation requests since approximately October 2024. During this time, she has 
observed a consistent pattern in the City’s responses to accommodation requests. According to 
Prado, the City has frequently delayed the process by requesting clarification regarding requests 
or by stating that no enforcement action was pending. When requests were submitted, the City 
often required medical verification, which BHU attempted to obtain from healthcare providers.

Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), 42 U.S.C. § 12131, et seq., applies 
to all programs and activities of state and local government. “[T]itle II applies to anything a 
public entity does.” 28 C.F.R. Part 35, App. B, Section 35.102; accord Barden v. City of 
Sacramento, 292 F.3d 1073, 1076 (9th Cir. 2002) (“the phrase 'services, programs, or activities' 
encompasses virtually everything that a public entity does”) (citing and quoting Johnson v. City 
of Saline, 151 F.3d 564, 569 (6th Cir. 1998); Lee v. City of L.A., 250 F.3d 668, 691 (9th Cir. 
2001) (“the ADA’s broad language brings within its scope ‘anything a public entity does.’”) 
(citation omitted). This means that the law’s prohibitions cover police activities and the 
enactment and enforcement of local ordinances including those relating to unhoused people. See, 
e.g., Sheehan v. City & Cty. of S.F., 743 F.3d 1211, 1232 (9th Cir. 2014), reversed in part on 
other grounds, City & Cnty. of San Francisco v. Sheehan, 135 S. Ct. 1765 (2015) (Title II covers 
arrests)
In the instant case, it appears that the City intends to seize vehicles without warrants and 
without considering alternatives to permanently depriving owners of their vehicles. [See 
Kiewietdejonge Decl.]

rule, stating in multiple responses that the requested accommodations would be denied regardless 
of the individual's disability or the nexus. [See, Prado Decl.] This alone establishes a likelihood 
of success on the merits.

B. The City Failed to Engage in a Good Faith Interactive Process
The ADA's implementing regulations require an "interactive process" that includes open 
dialogue and good faith efforts to identify and implement appropriate accommodations. See 28 
C.F.R. § 35.160(b)(2); Humphrey v. Mem'l Hosp. Ass'n, 239 F.3d 1128, 1137 (9th Cir. 2001). 
The City did the opposite.
Ms. Barbee's experience exemplifies this failure. She submitted a formal accommodation 
request through BHU and also spoke directly with Thomas Gregory during his outreach at the 
encampment. She "clearly told him my disabilities would be aggravated due the City's actions." 
(Barbee Decl. ¶ 4). Despite this direct communication, the City offered only a meaningless 
extension to March 10, which "doesn't address my disabilities. It doesn't protect me from another 
cardiac event. It doesn't give me a place to go or help with my belongings." (Barbee Decl. ¶ 5).

C. The City's Denials Create a Foreseeable Risk of Irreparable Harm, Including Death
The nexus between the City's actions and the harm to Plaintiffs is clear and direct. The 
declarations provide chilling accounts of the consequences.
Robbie Peddycoart describes a life-threatening heart condition: "I have aortic valve 
regurgitation. This means my aorta has been weakened by the arthritic process. It is not strong 
enough to complete the final pump of blood out of my heart, so blood crashes back into my 
heart. Stress makes this condition significantly worse." (Peddycoart Decl. ¶¶ 13-14). The risk is 
not speculative; it is familial: "My nephew... died on the spot" from stress-induced organ failure. 
"My grandmother... died at age 52. I am now 56, and I am at high risk of facing the same fate if I 
experience a stressful situation like an encampment abatement." (Peddycoart Decl. ¶¶ 15-16).
He also recounts a past arrest for his disability, proving the City's deliberate indifference: 
"I have been arrested before for being unable to move quickly enough during an enforcement 
action. Officers came at 5:45 a.m. ... I told them I needed an accommodation because I could not 
get up and move. I wasn't asking for special treatment—I was asking for basic understanding. 
They arrested me anyway." (Peddycoart Decl. ¶ 20).
Ms. Barbee's declaration is perhaps the most powerful evidence of foreseeable harm. She 
has already suffered a cardiac event during a City sweep. On October 4, 2022, during a previous 
enforcement action at 8th and Harrison, she went into cardiac arrest. The event was so severe 
that a Berkeley Police Officer, Schikore, intervened, telling her, "Alice, you don't look good. 
Come here, sit down on the ground." He blocked the sunlight from her eyes and called 
paramedics. (Barbee Decl. ¶¶ 10-12).

D. The City's Actions Constitute Discrimination "by Reason of" Disability
The City's denial of accommodations directly forces Plaintiffs into impossible situations 
because of their disabilities.
Ms. Barbee's experience illustrates this impossibility. Because she needs to sleep sitting 
up to breathe, the City's 9-square-foot rule and "two-person tent" requirement are not just 
inconvenient—they are medically impossible. "No one from the City that is asking me to comply 
with these rules has ever asked me how I sleep or what I need to prevent the aggravation of my 
disabilities." (Barbee Decl. ¶ 14).
Robbie Peddycoart summarizes the impossible choice: "If the City forces their activities 
on me, I may have to refuse to move or walk away, which I have done many times before. But if 
I walk away, I lose everything and I have literally nothing to shelter with or stay warm with 
overnight." (Peddycoart Decl. ¶ 23).
Thomas Barnett describes the physical impossibility of compliance: "Standing for even 
five minutes causes pain. The pain becomes unbearable, and I have to sit down and rest often... If

father before he dies." (Barnett Decl. ¶¶ 6d, 10e).
