I. INTRODUCTION 
On June 10, 2025, this Court granted limited injunctive relief for individuals still at risk of 
being evicted from the Harrison Corridor, to allow for completion of the interactive process under 
Title II of the Americans with Disabilities Act ("ADA"). Berkeley Homeless Union ("BHU") 
now seeks to relitigate the scope of this injunction, months after it was issued, and expand it to 
individuals who are not at risk of eviction from the Harrison Corridor. BHU does not explain why 
residents who have relocated outside of the Harrison Corridor need reasonable accommodations 
to comply with an order to relocate outside of the Harrison Corridor. Moreover, BHU fails to 
provide admissible evidence that the individuals it claims reside in the Harrison Corridor, even 
meet BHU' s definition of "reside." More concemingly, BHU is alarmingly unaware of key facts 
about its members, including the death of one member six months ago and the relocation of at 
least three other members to a different city entirely. Based upon this record, the only reasonable 
interpretation of the Court's order is that its limited injunctive relief applies only to seven BHU 
members with pending accommodation requests who were residing in the Harrison Corridor on 
June 10, 2025. 
II. ARGUMENT 
A. The Court's Order Clearly Applies Only to Individuals Currently Residing in the 
Harrison Corridor 
The City considers the Court's June 10, 2025 ruling from the bench and the subsequent 
Minute Order, [[ER 1863-1865 - Civil Minutes|ECF No. 104]], to be clear. The Court ordered "Limited Injunctive Relief," which 
applies to "those BHU members currently residing in the Harrison Corridor for whom BHU has 
already submitted a written accommodation request." Id. at 3 (Emphasis added). This language is 
not ambiguous-it sets temporal and geographic limits for who is covered by the injunction based 
on their status at the time the injunction was issued. At its most expansive, the furthest back the 
injunction reaches is 12:01 p.m., when the Court adjourned the hearing on June 10, 2025. Any 
individuals who were not residing in the Harrison Corridor at that time, or who were residing 
there but did not have a pending ADA accommodation request, are not covered by the Court's 
limited injunctive relief.

In supporting its motion, BHU relies heavily on two cases-Boyd v. City of San Rafael, 
No. 23-CV-04085-EMC, 2023 WL 6960368 (N.D. Cal. Oct. 19, 2023), and Santa Cruz Homeless 
Union v. Bernal, 514 F. Supp. 3d 1136 (N.D. Cal. 2021)-however, neither supports its proffered 
interpretation of the Court's order. BHU's case law stands for the banal conclusion that someone 
currently living in an encampment should be considered a resident of that encampment. But 
neither case supports the conclusion that an individual who is not living in an encampment, nor 
has been for months, should be considered "currently residing" in that encampment. In fact, Boyd 
. finds otherwise. 
In Boyd v. City of San Rafael, this Court found that several unhoused residents in that case 
"no longer reside" at the encampment because they were not presently located in the 
encampment. 2023 WL 6960368, at *10. For example, plaintiff Eddy Metz argued that he 
"remain[ed] part of [the encampment] though he has moved outside of the nuclear cluster of 
tents," but the Court concluded he was a non-resident. Id. Likewise, Shaleeta Boyd was found to 
be a non-resident when she testified that she had alternative housing and was no longer presently 
living in the encampment. Id. In other words, an individual's present location, not past or adjacent 
residence, establishes whether someone is considered a current resident of an encampment. 
Similarly, Santa Cruz Homeless Union v. Bernal makes clear that someone's current 
location in an encampment is properly their "place ofresidence." 514 F. Supp. 3d at 1142. There 
the court found that CDC's Guidelines during COVID-19 "instructs all persons to remain at their
