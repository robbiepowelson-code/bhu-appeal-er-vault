I. INTRODUCTION 
Plaintiff Berkeley Homeless Union (“BHU”) has an obligation to provide competent, 
admissible, timely evidence to support its summary judgment brief. See City’s Objections to 
Evidence. Because BHU has not done so, the Court should deny its summary judgment motion 
and grant Defendant City of Berkeley’s (“City’s”) cross-motion for summary judgment. 
In addition to not even attempting to meet its burden of producing evidence, BHU fails to 
engage with the City’s evidence in support of its motion in any meaningful way. BHU’s only 
response to the extensive factual record cited in the City’s paper is a false accusation that the 
City failed to answer questions posed by the Court, followed by BHU’s factually unsupported 
and legally dubious attempts to answer those questions. 
Of course, whatever “facts” may be in BHU’s brief are actually arguments of counsel. In 
many cases those arguments are unsubstantiated, but that is almost beside the point: the purpose 
of summary judgment is to allow the Court to consider evidence, including affidavits and other 
testimony submitted under oath, to determine if the moving party has met its burden of proof. 
Arguments of counsel cannot meet this burden with respect to BHU’s motion and cannot refute 
the prima facie showing made in the City’s brief. 
BHU’s disregard of the standards required for granting summary judgment is part of a 
larger pattern of ignoring rules and professional standards for appearing in this tribunal and 
hoping it will get away with it. BHU has failed to participate in good faith in the discovery 
process and has violated multiple Court orders as a result of its misconduct. With respect to the 
pending motions, every paper filed by BHU was late. The Court need not consider BHU’s 
untimely evidence, and under Rule 56(d), it would be error to grant summary judgment in 
BHU’s favor where it has willfully and prejudicially denied the City access to discovery. 
Finally, BHU’s attempts to respond to the City’s legal argument are equally scattered. In 
many cases, BHU did not respond to the City’s argument and has therefore waived its right to do 
so. In others, BHU’s responses are cursory and its citations to authorities easily distinguishable.

III. ARGUMENT 
A. BHU Has Failed to Submit Timely, Competent, Admissible Evidence to Support 
Its Motion and Opposition Brief. 
Because BHU bears the burden of proof at trial, it must produce competent evidence to 
establish a prima facie claim to avoid summary judgment. See Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 
317, 322–23 (1986). If the nonmoving party fails to produce this evidence, the Court should 
grant summary judgment. Nissan Fire & Marine Ins. Co. v. Fritz Companies, Inc., 210 F.3d 
1099, 1102 (9th Cir. 2000). As explained in the City’s Objections to BHU’s late-filed 
“Exhibits,” BHU has provided no timely-filed, competent evidence to support its motion or 
opposition brief. Because the City has met its burden of producing evidence to show that no 
triable issue of fact exists and BHU has submitted no admissible evidence in opposition to the 
City’s motion, summary judgment should enter for the City. 
BHU’s motion for summary judgment should also be denied under Federal Rule of Civil 
Procedure 56(d) because BHU has made a mockery of the discovery process in this case. Rule 
56(d) protects litigants who are denied the opportunity to seek discovery before a summary 
judgment motion is heard. Under Rule 56(d), the Court may defer consideration of or deny a 
motion for summary judgment “if a nonmovant shows by affidavit or declaration that, for 
specified reasons, it cannot present facts essential to justify its opposition.” Fed. R. Civ. P. 56(d); 
see also Sec. & Exch. Comm'n v. Stein, 906 F.3d 823, 833 (9th Cir. 2018). 
Due to BHU’s refusal to engage in discovery, the City cannot present facts to rebut 
BHU’s allegations, which are unsupported by evidence, particularly with respect to allegations

B. BHU Has Not Produced Evidence to Establish Organizational Standing. 
In opposing the City’s summary judgment motion on standing, BHU argues that it has 
satisfied all three prongs of the associational standing test. [[ER 1187-1211 - Plaintiff Berkeley Homeless Union’s Opposition to City of Berkeley’s M…|ECF No. 247]] (BHU Opp.) at 16-17. 
But BHU’s opposition is most notable for what it does not say. A plaintiff bears the burden to 
establish standing upfront and at every stage of the proceeding, including by satisfying every 
prong of the associational standing test. See Associated Gen. Contractors of Am. v. Cal. Dept. of 
Transp., 713 F.3d 1187, 1194 (9th Cir. 2013). BHU notably does not dispute that it bears this 
burden, or that to satisfy it, BHU must introduce admissible evidence, for each form of 
accommodation its purported members seek and were denied by the City. See Access 4 All, Inc. 
v. Trump Int’l Hotel & Tower Condo., 458 F. Supp. 2d 160, 174-75 (S.D.N.Y. 2006). BHU 
claims that it has met its burden by submitting a few months-old declarations, a joint status 
report, and an absent declaration from a nurse practitioner. [[ER 1187-1211 - Plaintiff Berkeley Homeless Union’s Opposition to City of Berkeley’s M…|ECF No. 247]] at 16-17.
The Court need not comb through the record to find evidence to support BHU’s motion. 
But even considering the evidence submitted earlier in the case, the Parnell ([[ER 1782-1785 - Declaration of Lewanda Parnell ISO of Plaintiff’s Motion for Order to…|ECF No. 121]]), 
Muwakkil ([[ER 1774-1781 - Declaration of Shareef Muwakkil ISO of Plaintiff’s Motion for Order to…|ECF No. 123]]), and White ([[ER 1768-1773 - Declaration of Austin White ISO of Plaintiff’s Motion for Order to Sho…|ECF No. 125]]) declarations state only that these 
individuals have mobility limitations, and that Mr. Muwakkil and Mr. White have mental 
conditions that make moving stressful. This evidence does not establish standing for every 
accommodation BHU has requested. For instance, none of these individuals claims to own a 
vehicle, so none has standing to seek exemption from parking restrictions. And necessary facts

1. BHU fails to establish a prima facie claim for relief under the ADA. 
BHU cannot establish a prima facie failure-to-accommodate claim under Title II. To 
prevail, a plaintiff must demonstrate: (1) a qualifying disability; (2) that the public entity knew 
of the disability; and (3) that the entity failed to provide a reasonable accommodation necessary 
to avoid discrimination on the basis of disability. See McGary v. City of Portland (9th Cir. 2004)

Vinson, 288 F.3d at 1154.

generally applicable regulations. It prohibits exclusion from, or denial of benefits of, services

programmatic rules, and where a requested accommodation would fundamentally alter the 
program or impose an undue administrative burden, and the head of the public entity denies the 
request with a written explanation, there is no discrimination as a matter of law. 28 C.F.R. 
§§ 35.130(b)(7), 35.150(a)(3).
