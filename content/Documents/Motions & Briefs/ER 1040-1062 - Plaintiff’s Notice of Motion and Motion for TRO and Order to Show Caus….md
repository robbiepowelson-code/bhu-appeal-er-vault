MEMORANDUM OF POINTS AND AUTHORITIES 
I. INTRODUCTION 
The City of Berkeley has issued notice of its intent to close the 8th and Harrison encampment. 
The City ordered all encampment residents to vacate the encampment and the surrounding 1/3 mile 
radius by March 9, 2026 due to an outbreak of leptospirosis, but it has not identified a safe place where 
residents can relocate. The City also intends to destroy all “contaminated soft goods” in extended 
contact with the ground, such as blankets and tents, and all property remaining in the noticed location on 
or after March 9, 2026. Although the City declared that it is too “hazardous” for City employees to sort 
through items from the encampment for storage, encampment residents will be forced to sort through 
items themselves or risk losing essential survival gear including tents, bedding, clothing, medication, 
and mobility aids. The City has not identified a safe relocation site or offered to replace contaminated 
survival equipment, which means that residents and their potentially contaminated belongings and 
vehicles will scatter throughout the City, spreading the bacteria and leaving residents without shelter, 
community support, and access to essential service providers. 
Absent immediate relief, Plaintiffs and Putative Class members will suffer immediate harm, 
including loss of shelter and essential survival gear and exposure to the elements. Plaintiffs seek a 
temporary restraining order to prevent the City from proceeding with the closure in a manner that 
violates Plaintiffs’ Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights and their rights under the California 
Constitution.

transmission and the dangers to humans and animals. Doohan Decl. ¶¶ 4-7. According to Dr. Doohan, 
leptospirosis is a bacterial disease that lives in the kidneys and blood of animals, particularly rats. Id. ¶ 4. 
It is spread through infected animals’ urine which enters the environment through soil, mud, or water. 
Id. Accumulations of water, including puddles and other standing water present at encampments, can 
spread Leptospirosis from animals to humans. Id. ¶¶ 9, 10, 16-17. Humans and animals may become 
infected when contaminated water or soil touches a cut or scrape or their eyes, nose, or mouth. Id. ¶ 4. 
According to Dr. Doohan, in humans, leptospirosis can cause high fever, headache, severe calf 
and leg pain, and conjunctivitis. Id. ¶ 5. Without treatment, leptospirosis in humans can progress to 
kidney damage, meningitis, liver failure, trouble breathing, and death. Id. Leptospirosis is particularly 
dangerous for pregnant women and fetuses. Id. ¶ 6. In dogs, leptospirosis causes excessive thirst, 
vomiting, shivering, and lethargy. Id. ¶ 7. Without treatment, leptospirosis in dogs can cause liver failure 
and death. Id. Leptospirosis vaccines are available for dogs but not humans. Id. ¶ 8. 
C. The forced relocation of encampment residents—and planned enforcement actions 
that make no provision for the sanitation or replacement of their tents, bedding, 
vehicles, or other essential items—make little sense from a public health perspective, 
and will only spread leptospirosis to other areas of the City. 
The City has taken the position that it will not “sort through or store” any items from the 
Harrison Street encampment, and that any items left in the encampment will be summarily destroyed, 
because the “presence of Leptospirosis” makes handling of these items too “hazardous.” Roznowski 
Decl., Exh. B at 2-3. It has also instructed that all “contaminated soft goods” such as blankets and tents 
that have been in “extended contact with the ground” must be destroyed, and stated that vehicles with 
“long-term rat infestations” are “irremediable biohazards” that must similarly be destroyed. Id. at 13. 
The City has no plan to provide residents with replacement tents, bedding, or other survival gear, 
and does not appear to have any plan to determine whether a vehicle actually has a “long-term rat 
infestation,” or whether such an infestation, if it exists, is in fact an “irremediable biohazard.” The City 
should be barred from destroying any vehicles unless it has specific, irrefutable evidence to support both 
points.

D. Harm to Class Members and Dangers
The City’s proposed course of action will cause irreparable harm to the Putative Class. Rather 
than work collaboratively with encampment residents to keep them safe from infection, the City of 
Berkeley ordered residents to leave the encampment and surrounding 1/3 mile radius. See Roznowski 
Decl., Exh. B. The City insists that residents’ belongings are too hazardous for City employees to touch, 
yet instructed residents to take their potentially contaminated essential survival gear with them into the 
community—without providing residents with any personal protective equipment or sanitation supplies 
to mitigate the claimed risk of handling these “hazardous” possessions. Id. This is so even though the 
City’s own documents recommend that “when contacting mud, standing water or items exposed to 
mud/water in the encampment,” people should “use waterproof boots, masks, protective eyewear and 
nitrile/rubber gloves,” and “[a]void skin, mouth, nose, or eye contact with mud, runoff, or unclean 
water.” Id. at 13. The City does not plan to provide any such protective equipment, and it is unclear how 
unhoused individuals with no resources—particularly those with disabilities or other health conditions 
that make them especially vulnerable—are expected to procure such equipment. If residents leave

consequence of [its] action.’” Prado v. City of Berkeley, No. 23-cv-04537, 2024 WL 3697037, at *28

party’s constitutional rights.”). The equities therefore favor a temporary restraining order because the 
City’s proposed action appears to increase rather than decrease health risks to 8th and Harrison 
residents, and because of the strong public interest in “maintaining the protections afforded by the 
Constitution to those most in need of such protection.” Cobine v. City of Eureka, No. C 16-02239, 2016 
WL 1730084, at *7 (N.D. Cal. May 2, 2016); see also Santa Cruz Homeless Union, 514 F. Supp. 3d at 
1146 (same). 
E. Plaintiffs Should Not Be Required to Post Bond.
The Court should waive any requirement to post a bond. Plaintiffs and the class are indigent 
unhoused people with no means to post any bond. When issuing an injunction, this Court has complete 
“discretion as to the amount of security required, if any.” Barahona-Gomez v. Reno, 167 F.3d 1228, 
1237 (9th Cir. 1999) (emphasis added). That is because the district court is in the best position to 
determine whether a bond is required. Id. Where there is little to no potential harm to the Defendant 
enjoined, little or no bond is needed. See id. Enjoining a government defendant from enforcing its 
policies often evinces no harm to that entity. Id. (enjoinment of government’s immigration policy would 
result in minimal cost to the government). Furthermore, where public policy favors an injunction, and 
plaintiffs lack the means to pay a bond, the bond requirement should be waived or be nominal. Id.
Courts have also waived the bond requirement where, when weighing the hardship to the plaintiff in 
making the bond versus the possible harm to the defendant in issuing the injunction, the balance of these 
equities weighs overwhelmingly in favor of the party seeking the injunction. See Youth Justice Coal. v. 
City of Los Angeles, 264 F. Supp. 3d 1057, 1073 (C.D. Cal. 2017) (citing precedent, and dispensing with 
a bond where plaintiff could not afford to pay it). Also, if plaintiffs show a strong likelihood of success 
on the merits, the security may be waived. Scherr v. Volpe, 466 F.2d 1027, 1035 (7th Cir. 1972); Colin 
ex rel. Colin v. Orange Unified Sch. Dist., 83 F. Supp. 2d 1135, 1151 (C.D. Cal. 2000) (waiving bond).
