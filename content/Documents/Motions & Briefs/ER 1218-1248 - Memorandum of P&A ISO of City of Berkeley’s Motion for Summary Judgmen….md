I. INTRODUCTION
Defendants bring this summary judgment motion pursuant to the Court’s recent order setting 
an expedited briefing schedule. ECF 202. The City objects to the Court’s decision to compel the City 
to move for summary judgment on two-weeks' notice, delineate the precise points that must be 
addressed, and set an expedited briefing schedule when discovery has not closed and the dispositive 
motion deadline is not for several months. 
Without waiving these objections, the City brings this motion as ordered and is entitled to 
summary judgment on all of Berkeley Homeless Union (“BHU”)’s claims. First, BHU lacks 
standing. At the summary judgment stage it can no longer rely on mere allegations, and it has not met 
its burden to identify, for each form of relief a specific member with standing for its ADA claims, nor 
has it offered concrete evidence to support its standing allegations for the constitutional claims. 
Second, regarding BHU’s ADA claims, the City engaged in good faith interactive process discussions 
with BHU for almost a year and, as a result, granted reasonable accommodations, offered alternatives 
where feasible, and explained when requests were not required under Title II of the ADA. As such, 
there is no dispute of material fact that the City complied with the ADA. Third, as the Court already 
recognized at the preliminary injunction stage, BHU cannot support its claim that the City subjects its 
members to state created danger. The City has not placed campers in any danger that is not inherent 
to being unhoused, and BHU’s relied-upon harm is either purely hypothetical or factually incorrect. 
Lastly, BHU has not established that the City has seized its property in violation of the Fourth 
Amendment. There is no dispute that the City provided more than the required notice and any 
property seized was stored or discarded in accordance with established law.

This testing history not only confirms the presence of Leptospirosis in rats and dogs at the 
encampment, but it also shows that conditions have materially worsened at the encampment, meaning 
that residents face greater harm if they are allowed to stay. That is because the conditions of the 
encampment now present the risk of contracting Leptospirosis from infected animal urine and body 
fluids. Id. ¶ 17. These conditions create an elevated risk of Leptospirosis spreading from infected 
animal urine and body fluids to humans. Id. Without treatment, Leptospirosis in people can progress 
to kidney damage, meningitis, liver failure, trouble breathing, and even death. Id. 
Addressing the presence of Leptospirosis requires eradication of the rodent population. 
Declaration of Ronald Torres (“Torres Dec.”) ¶ 4. Eradication entails a multi-step process of placing 
live traps, CO2 treatment of burrows, and ultimately rodenticide bait. Id. ¶ 8. This process, in turn, 
requires the destruction or removal of all rodent harborage conditions, which include: uncontained 
food, including pet food; makeshift structures near or on top of burrows; piles of building materials 
(such as pallets, tarps, or blankets), personal belongings, debris, or clothing; mattress or stuffed 
furniture, and long-term parked vehicles. Id. ¶¶ 5-6. Thus, eradiation cannot be effectively 
implemented if any individuals remain encamped in the area because their personal belongings will 
constitute rodent harborage. Id. ¶¶ 9-10. Even one person living in a tent or structure on the sidewalk, 
or in one long-term vehicle, constitutes a rodent harborage that will act as a sanctuary and reservoir 
from which rats can repopulate the Harrison Corridor. Id. 
IV. LEGAL STANDARD
Summary judgment is proper “if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and 
admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any 
material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” Celotex Corp. v. 
Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 317 (1986); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. The moving party “bears the initial”

Finally, BHU also fails to meet the third prong of the state-created danger test because a city’s 
alleged lack of social services does not amount to the City acting with deliberate indifference. While 
the “general circumstances of being homeless [] cannot be minimized,” courts have explained that 
there can be no viable state-created danger claim “without allegations of intentional eviction during 
precarious weather or other facts indicating deliberate indifference to the safety and welfare of the 
population.” Cobine v. City of Eureka, 250 F. Supp. 3d 423, 433 (N.D. Cal. 2017); see also Reed v. 
City of Emeryville, 568 F. Supp. 3d 1029, 1040 (N.D. Cal. 2021); see also Young v. City of Los 
Angeles, No. CV2000709JFWRAO, 2020 WL 616363, at *7 (C.D. Cal. Feb. 10, 2020). 
(“Additionally, Plaintiff's general complaints that the City is not doing enough to assist his social 
needs [] are not sufficient to state a substantive due process claim as there is no affirmative right to 
governmental aid.”) For instance, in Cobine, Plaintiffs alleged that the closure of their encampment 
community forced them to move to “unfamiliar areas”, “without the support of the community”, and 
made them “vulnerable to assault, theft, harassment, and worse.” Cobine, 250 F. Supp. at 433. 
Although the Court accepted that the encampment closure disbanded plaintiffs’ community 
environment, it dismissed their state-created danger claim because it sought relief only for the 
“generalized dangers of living on the street” which “preexisted Plaintiffs’ relocation,” and did not 
amount to “a deliberate indifference of placing Plaintiffs in an inherently more dangerous situation 
than they had faced previously.” Id. Similarly, in Reed, while Plaintiffs alleged that the congregate 
shelter might exacerbate their mental health, such a claim did not mean that the City placed plaintiffs 
in a more dangerous situation than they would be in while living in an encampment next to an active 
construction site. Reed v. City of Emeryville, 568 F. Supp. 3d 1029, 1040 (N.D. Cal. 2021). In this 
case, Plaintiffs have not introduced any evidence to the record that closing the encampment amounts 
to more than the “generalized danger of living on the street” that requires movement to unfamiliar 
areas, which alone is insufficient to show that the City acted with deliberate indifference. 
Accordingly, BHU has not established and cannot establish that the City has subjected its 
members to a state created danger.

E. BHU’s Property Destruction Claims Fail 
The Court should also grant summary judgment on BHU’s property destruction claims. BHU 
has not, and cannot, demonstrate that any members have had property seized in violation of the 
Fourth Amendment. The City’s policies and practices for encampment abatements not only comply 
with the requirements of the Fourth Amendment and the California Constitution, they many respects

Municipalities are not categorically barred from destroying items left on streets; if unsheltered 
individuals are provided with meaningful notice and an opportunity to be heard, there are no Fourth 
or Fourteenth Amendment violations. Lavan v. City of Los Angeles, 693 F.3d 1022, 1032 (9th Cir. 
2012); see also Janosko v. City of Oakland, No. 3:23-CV-00035-WHO, 2023 WL 3029256, at *3-4

destroy property that is hazardous, clearly garbage, or reasonably believed to be abandoned. Lavan,
