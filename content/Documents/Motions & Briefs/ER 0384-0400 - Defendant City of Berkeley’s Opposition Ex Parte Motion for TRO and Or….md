I. INTRODUCTION 
The Court should deny Plaintiff Berkeley Homeless Union’s (“BHU’s”) second Ex Parte 
Motion For Temporary Restraining Order And Order To Show Cause Why City Of Berkeley And 
ADA Coordinator Thomas Gregory Should Not Be Held In Contempt, [[ER 0592-0613 - Plaintiff Berkeley Homeless Union’s Ex Parte Motion for TRO and Order…|ECF No. 264]]. 
Defendant City of Berkeley (“City”) first noticed the closure of the Harrison Street 
encampment in January 2025 and at that time began preparing in earnest for the partial closure that 
was noticed for March 9, 2026. Those preparations included extensive public health outreach to 
address the presence of leptospirosis (a disease caused by bacteria) at the encampment and a prior 
notice of partial abatement posted in February 2026, which was rescinded by the City due to the 
expectation of inclement weather at the time of the abatement action. On March 10, in response to 
Plaintiff’s second TRO Application, the Court issued an order enjoining the City from taking any 
abatement actions against certain individuals who submitted ADA accommodation requests pending 
hearing on BHU’s second TRO application, set for March 11 at 11:00 a.m. 
The Court has already rejected Plaintiff’s first attempt to seek an ex parte application to 
block the noticed partial abatement. As with its first TRO attempt, the Court should deny this second 
TRO application because, as it previously observed, Plaintiff has failed to submit evidence that “the 
City has failed to satisfy its obligations under the ADA or that any particular accommodation request 
has been unlawfully denied.” ECF No. 260. Plaintiff once again fails to meet its burden of proof in 
this second, procedurally improper application. Plaintiff submits only 7 declarations in support of 
its application, far fewer than the many individuals who have sought accommodation. Plaintiff fails 
to raise any substantive objections to the City’s responses to reasonable accommodation made by 
encampment residents under the Americans with Disability Act (“ADA”). Instead, without citing 
authority, they appear to argue that it violates federal law to have the City’s ADA Coordinator send 
out ADA determinations following outreach and an interactive process. This argument has no merit. 
Plaintiff submits declarations that ignore the City’s extensive efforts to review and respond 
to Plaintiff’s requests for accommodation, as explained in detail in the Declaration of the City’s 
ADA Coordinator, Thomas Gregory (“Gregory Decl.”) [[ER 0637-0658 - Declaration of Thomas Gregory re Completion of ADA Interactive Process…|ECF No. 261]]. Mr. Gregory demonstrates 
that the City engaged in extensive outreach to numerous individuals about their accommodation

II. BACKGROUND 
Facts relevant to Plaintiff’s second request for a temporary restraining order to block the 
partial abatement of the Harrison Street encampment are detailed in the City’s opposition to 
Plaintiffs’ motion for a temporary restraining order in the related case, Prado, et al. v. City of 
Berkeley, USDC ND Case No. 23-cv-04537-EMC. Prado [[ER 1633-1644 - Discovery Dispute Letter Brief|ECF No. 185]]. The Court granted the 
Prado Plaintiffs’ request in part and denied the request in part, granting relief that was limited to 
City plans to abate the public health hazards caused by the presence of approximately six RVs at 
the encampment. The Court denied the request for broader relief under the Fourth and Fourteenth 
Amendments, citing long-standing precedent that allows the government to destroy property without 
just compensation when necessary to do so to protect public health or safety. See id. at 3.

vacate the encampment on or before March 9. The Court limited that order by imposing restrictions

arrangements for replacement tents and sleeping bags to be made available to encampment residents 
who wish to take them.

a clear showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such relief.” Winter v. Nat. Res. Def. Council, Inc., 
555 U.S. 7, 22 (2008). In the Ninth Circuit, the same standard governs requests for temporary 
restraining orders and preliminary injunctions. See Stuhlbarg Int’l Sales Co. v. John D. Brush & 
Co., 240 F.3d 832, 839 n.7 (9th Cir. 2001).

IV. ARGUMENT 
A. The Court Should Reject Plaintiff’s Procedurally Improper Ex Parte 
Application. 
Local Rule 65-1 establishes the procedures for filing a motion seeking a temporary 
restraining order. In pertinent part, the Rule requires (1) separate memorandum of points and 
authorities in support of the motion, (2) a proposed order and order to show cause, and (3) a 
declaration by counsel certifying that notice has been provided to the opposing party or explaining 
why such notice could not be provided. N.D Cal. L.R. 65-1. 
Plaintiff complied with none of these requirements. See EFC No. 264. Plaintiff’s ex parte 
application is improper. See N.D. Cal. L.R. 7-1 (“Unless otherwise ordered by the assigned Judge, 
a party may file an ex parte motion, that is, a motion filed without notice to opposing party, only if 
a statute, Federal Rule, local rule, or Standing Order authorizes ex parte filing.”). Plaintiff’s filing 
of a last-minute application is highly prejudicial to the City. The Court should enforce the plain 
language of its procedural rules and deny Plaintiff’s application for failing to comply with them. 
B. Plaintiff Cannot Prevail On The Merits Of Its ADA Claim. 
Plaintiff’s second application for a temporary restraining order fails to address the factual or 
legal grounds for the City’s responses to the newly identified ADA claimants. Both of these defects 
provide alternative grounds for denying Plaintiff’s application.

C. Plaintiff Is Not Likely To Succeed Under The Fourteenth Amendment State 
Created Danger Theory 
Plaintiff’s state-created danger theory fails because the City has not placed unhoused 
individuals in any danger that is not inherent to being unhoused. “To succeed on a state-created 
danger claim, a plaintiff must establish that (1) a state actor’s affirmative actions created or exposed 
him to an actual, particularized danger that he would not otherwise would have faced, (2) that the 
injury he suffered was foreseeable, and (3) that the state actor was deliberately indifferent to the 
known danger.” Sinclair v. City of Seattle, 61 F.4th 674, 680 (9th Cir. 2023) (quotation marks 
omitted). “In examining whether [the City] affirmatively places an individual in danger, [a court 
must] examine whether the [City] left the person in a situation that was more dangerous than the 
one in which they found [them].” Kennedy v. City of Ridgefield, 439 F.3d 1055, 1062 (9th Cir. 
2006).
