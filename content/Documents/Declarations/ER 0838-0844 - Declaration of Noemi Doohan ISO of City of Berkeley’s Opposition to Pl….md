6. In my professional judgment, the conditions at the Harrison Corridor encampment

conditions support uncontrolled growth of rodent populations and related increase in potential 
contact between humans and rats, contaminated water, surfaces, or materials. 
7. Eradication of the rats in the Harrison Corridor, as opposed to suppression or control, 
is necessary to control the spread of Leptospirosis because the bacteria is spread by rat body 
fluids, including urine, and is able to remain alive in the soil and any standing water for up to 30 
days, and potentially longer depending on conditions. Any rats remaining in and around the 
encampment are likely to be continually exposed and potentially infected by this residual bacteria 
in the environment. Those remaining infected rats will then produce more contagion through their 
urine, starting the cycle of infection all over again.

15. I have personally visited and observed conditions at the Harrison Street encampment

Based on my observations and my experience addressing rodent-borne disease risks in 
urban environments, stationary vehicles can create conditions that allow rodents to harbor 
underneath or around them, particularly when the vehicles remain unmoved for long periods and 
when surrounding materials provide nesting opportunities and food sources. Areas beneath 
vehicles that remain in place for extended periods can also make it difficult for vector control 
personnel to inspect for burrows or place traps or kill rats with poisonous substances. For these 
reasons, stationary RVs and other vehicles that remain in the same location for extended periods 
can contribute to environmental conditions that support rodent activity and associated public 
health risks. Moving these vehicles allows City staff and vector control personnel to access 
previously obstructed ground surfaces and address rodent harborage conditions.

17. In addition to increasing the likelihood that a fire may start or spread rapidly, the

encampment residents, emergency responders, and the surrounding community.

rodent harborage, particularly when food waste, stored belongings, or structural gaps allow 
rodents to nest within or beneath the vehicle. 
20. In order to effectively address rodent harborage and environmental contamination,

22. Plaintiffs assert that requiring individuals to leave the encampment will spread 
leptospirosis throughout the City. Uncontrolled growth of rat populations in an environment 
known to have had transmission of Leptospirosis from rats to dogs is the primary concern for 
spread of Leptospirosis in this area. Infected rats are the primary vector for the spread of 
leptospirosis in humans, dogs and other animals. People, their dogs, or their possessions are not 
the primary vector for the spread of leptospirosis. Relocating the property of encampment 
residents does not present the same level of public health risk as an uncontrolled rat population 
being allowed to continue to grow in the setting of leptospirosis. While there is risk in handling 
possessions that could be contaminated with leptospirosis, the city has had an ongoing program in 
place to dispose of those belongings at the encampment. Addressing the environmental conditions 
posed by rats in this encampment requires access to the affected area so that sanitation, rodent 
control, and environmental remediation measures can be implemented. 
23. Allowing the encampment to remain in place would prevent the City and its public 
health and vector control personnel from accessing areas where rodent burrows and 
contamination are present and would allow those environmental conditions to persist. 
Temporarily relocating individuals and vehicles from the affected area allows the City to access 
the site, implement rodent abatement measures, and address the environmental conditions 
contributing to the presence and spread of leptospirosis.
