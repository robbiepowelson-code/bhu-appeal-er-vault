I, Ronald Torres, declare as follows:
1. I have personal knowledge of the facts described herein. If called as a witness, I could
and would testify competently and truthfully to the facts set forth in this declaration. 
2. I have been continuously employed as the Environmental Health Division Manager for
the City of Berkeley since October of 2018. As part of my job duties I oversee a staff of 
Registered Environmental Health Specialists and a Vector Control Technician who are trained 
to identify and abate a variety of environmental health hazards in the community as well as in 
regulated businesses. I participate in meetings and certain activities of the City’s Homeless 
Response Team (“HRT”), a multi-departmental group of City staff working together to address 
the needs of the unhoused population in Berkeley. Through my interactions with the HRT, I 
understand that the City intends to close the encampment located in the area referred to as the 
Harrison Corridor. 
3. The City of Berkeley coordinates its vector control operations with Alameda County
Vector Control. Berkeley Vector Control has recently specifically started to coordinate with 
Alameda County Vector Control to eradicate the rats in the Harrison Corridor in response to 
the recent discovery of Leptospirosis in rats and dogs associated with the encampment. 
4. The Harrison Corridor is the only place in the Alameda County identified with
confirmed Leptospirosis, and rats are the primary vector to spread Leptospirosis so complete 
eradication of the rat population is necessary. 
5. Eradication, as opposed to suppression or control, means the capture or killing of as
many rats as possible and the destruction or removal of rodent harborage conditions. 
6. Rodent harborage means an area of other than a burrow where a rodent is likely to
forage for or bring food to, or remain in safety. Rodent harborage conditions include: 
uncontained food, including pet food; makeshift structures near or on top of burrows; piles of 
building materials (such as pallets, tarps, or blankets), personal belongings, debris, or clothing; 
mattress or stuffed furniture. Long-term parked vehicles are also common rodent harborage 
locations if the vehicle has access points for the rats to enter, as rats are known to crawl into 
vehicle engines to chew on the wires.

7. Eradication of the rats in the Harrison Corridor, as opposed to suppression or control, is
necessary to control the Leptospirosis because the bacteria is able to remain alive in the soil 
and any standing water for up to 30 days. Any rats remaining in and around the encampment 
are likely to be continually infected by this residual bacteria. Those remaining infected rats will 
then produce more contagion through their urine, starting the cycle of infection all over again.
8. Eradication will take multiple operations to trap and kill rats. The intended method of
eradication is as follows: First, Berkeley and Alameda County Vector Control must perform a 
survey of the Harrison Corridor to identify locations of most intensive rodent activity to set
live traps. Alameda County Vector Control will set live traps for approximately 24 hours, 
collect the captured rats, test them, and destroy them. This trapping operation will be repeated 
on a weekly basis. In between these trapping events, Berkeley Vector Control will support 
reduction of the rat population using CO2 treatment of the burrows if requested by the county,.
After the live trapping events reflect a suitable reduction in the rat population, Alameda 
County Vector Control will follow up with rodenticide bait to enable the most thorough 
eradication effort of any residual rat population.
9. The plan described in the preceding paragraph cannot be effectively carried out if any
individuals are allowed to remain encamped on the sidewalk in the Harrison Corridor for the 
following reasons:
First, any tents, other structures, or collections of personal belongings remaining 
on the sidewalk will prevent vector control personnel from definitively 
identifying rodent burrow locations or systematically placing live traps.
Second, any tents or other structures, or collections of personal belongings 
remaining on the sidewalk may block full access to rodent burrows, preventing 
the use of rodenticide treatment and/or CO2 treatment. While the burrows 
themselves may be located on private property abutting the sidewalk, vector 
control personnel need physical access to the burrows. Not only do the tents, 
other structures, and collections of personal belongings physically block access 
in some locations, but also the presence of used needles, human and animal

Third, the continued presence of rodent harborage conditions and food makes it 
impossible to control the rats because rats do not remain in their burrows. If any 
rodent harborage conditions exist, rats will be present in the harborage areas
instead of their burrows, and so CO2 treatment of the burrows will not kill 
them. Live trapping alone is very unlikely to be sufficient to eradicate the rat 
population if harborage conditions—especially where food will be available—
remain in the Harrison Corridor. Even one person living in a tent or structure on 
the sidewalk, or in one long-term vehicle, constitutes a rodent harborage that 
will act as a sanctuary and reservoir from which rats can repopulate the 
Harrison Corridor.
Fourth, Alameda County Vector Control cannot use rodenticide in areas of the 
encampment if there is any encampment in the area because the poison presents 
a danger to human health and to pets. It is also because the poisoned rat 
carcasses need to be collected as quickly and thoroughly as possible to avoid 
non-targeted kills, such as birds of prey or other wildlife that might consume the 
carcasses. The presence of encampments, especially where hazards such as used 
needles are present, makes it both dangerous and unreasonably difficult to 
collect the poisoned carcasses.

BHU members or other encampment residents. However, even if this is true, it is the rodent 
harborage conditions and forage opportunities within the encampment itself that support the rat 
population. Any surviving portion of the population is likely to spread back throughout the 
encampment within a matter of weeks, which means Leptospirosis is likely to still be present in 
the soil and standing water when the rats return. As a result, if even just two or three 
individuals are allowed to remain in the Harrison Corridor, the City and County vector control

12. If people are allowed to remain within the Harrison Corridor, at a minimum from a

13. Regular, transient vehicle parking is not an issue because those vehicles do not
represent harborage conditions; it is the long-term stationary vehicles that present rodent 
harborage. 
14. The rats within the Harrison Corridor are known to me to be a biting hazard. City
personnel who handle any of the rodent harborage materials as defined above are at risk of 
being bitten. Standard PPE that is reasonably available to City employees and can be 
practicably used by street maintenance crews does not protect employees from rat bites, or 
exposure to the Leptospirosis bacteria when handling contaminated materials. The risk of 
Leptospirosis infection from such rat bites, and potential contact with rat urine significantly 
increases if City employees are required to physically sort through or store contaminated 
objects, and would require protective gear and equipment beyond the standard PPE which is 
reasonably available to the City. I am not aware of any protective gear known to adequately 
protect employees from this type of biological contamination and potential bites from animal 
vectors. The closest analog of appropriate PPE that I am aware of that the City could 
potentially utilize are the full-body protective suits the City uses for yellow-jacket nest 
removal. These suits are costly, and not designed to be bite-proof or biologically impermeable 
for this purpose, and therefore would provide no assurance of employee safety from rat bites.
