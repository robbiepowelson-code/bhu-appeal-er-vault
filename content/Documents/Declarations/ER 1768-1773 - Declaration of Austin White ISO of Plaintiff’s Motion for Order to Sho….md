1. My name is Austin White, and I have been a resident of Berkeley, California since 2011.

half of my life. I am 50 years old. I’m a member of the Berkeley Homeless Union.

2. For the past two and a half years, I have lived at the encampment located at 8th and 
Harrison Streets. Before that, I stayed at the Golden Bear Inn, a permanent supportive 
housing site, until I was wrongfully evicted. Prior to that, I camped at People’s Park and 
along the I-80 freeway ramp near University Avenue. Every encampment where I have 
lived in Berkeley has been closed.
3. I live with multiple disabilities, including esophageal varices, a condition I have had 
since I was three years old. This condition makes it medically dangerous for me to lift 
objects over 20 pounds. I also have PTSD and other mental health conditions that affect 
my ability to manage stress, change and engage with authority figures.
4. On July 11, 2025, I attended an in-person meeting with the City of Berkeley’s ADA 
Coordinator and other city officials to discuss my request for reasonable accommodations 
under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). I was accompanied by officers of the
Berkeley Homeless Union, Yesica Prado and Gordon Gilmore, and officer Robbie 
Powelson of the California Homeless Union and LifeLong Medical Nurse Practitioner 
Olivia DeBree, who were there to support me at the meeting.
5. I entered the meeting in good faith, expecting a two-way dialogue about accommodations 
that would allow me to avoid harm and maintain access to all city programs provided at 
the encampment, which are essential resources to my survival.
6. Instead, I was met with City staff reading statements from their computers, denying my 
requests without considering my specific disabilities and deflecting questions. Anything 
that was proposed was just me with a no. I asked questions, but none were answered. I 
did not feel heard. At one point, I said, “I just want to be treated like a human being, not

7. At the meeting, I shared the extreme physical and emotional stress I live under every day

10. I made several requests during the meeting including:
I asked to be allowed to stay where I am temporarily, until the City can identify a 
stable, safe place for me to go. I said, “Where I am right now: I’m safe and I feel 
comfortable.” I keep my camping area clean and respectable. 
11. I asked where I’m supposed to go if this abatement happens. I said, “If this abatement 
happens, then where am I going to go? To jail? Again?” I’ve been homeless over 30 
years and still have no idea where I can exist without being punished. I often question: 
where do I go in the city that I call home? There’s nowhere left to go.
12. I asked to keep all of my essential property, including my house and supplies for my 
emotional support animals. My tent is my home and critical to my health and survival. I 
don’t have a door I can keep locked, but I can zip up for my personal safety.
13. I asked to keep access to services at my current location, including bathrooms, trash 
pickup and outreach workers. I rely on County medical services and housing navigation 
that come to this encampment. If I’m moved, I’ll lose that support. I would have to start 
over with nothing once again. It has happened so much, I am starting to feel defeated to 
keep trying and living.
14. The City did not offer me any alternatives. I asked directly to Thomas Gregory, “Where 
am I supposed to go? What am I supposed to do? What’s your suggestions?” No one 
answered. One of the City Attorneys finally said, “Mr. White, unfortunately I have to 
repeat what we’ve continued to say is we’re not in a position to answer that question.”
15. I felt helpless and angry, so I left the building to return to my dogs. I left because I felt 
completely dismissed and like I was just wasting my time explaining things over and over

16. Several things stood out to me during the meeting: Mr. Gregory admitted that sweeps are

disabilities work. They expect me to lift, pack, and move everything I own with very little 
notice of 24 hours, something I physically cannot do without risking a medical 
emergency. The nine-square-foot limit makes it impossible for me to sleep safely with 
my gear and my dogs. And when they take my property, they don’t treat it as medically 
necessary, city staff just throw it away. I’ve lost clothes, shoes, food, medication, bike, 
therapeutic art supplies, dog food, blankets, bed, musical instruments, tent — my whole
