Dear Mr. Gregory,
I am writing on behalf of Brenda Bolares, a member of the Berkeley Homeless Union, to formally request a reasonable
accommodation under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) due to her learning and cognitive disabilities.
Ms. Bolares faces significant challenges in completing daily life tasks in an outdoor environment, particularly those
that require problem-solving, decision-making, and adaptation to frequent change. Due to her disability-related
impairments, relocating would create an unreasonable hardship and exacerbate her difficulties in maintaining
stability, accessing resources, and performing essential functions.
Requested ADA Accommodations:
1) Permission to remain in place with her tent until an ADA-compliant shelter is available – Due to her cognitive
disability, frequent displacement prevents her from maintaining stability and worsens her ability to function
independently.
2) Exemption from relocation requirements that would force her into an unstable and overwhelming situation –
Relocation increases her cognitive overload, making it nearly impossible for her to perform basic survival tasks.
3) Protection of her tent as a necessary accommodation – Her tent provides a structured, familiar environment that
helps her manage her disability-related challenges in an already difficult situation.
4) Exemption from citation and arrest related to the abatement order – Because her disability prevents her from
complying with relocation orders and her dwelling was not documented in the city’s abatement report as
imposing a health and safety concern, enforcing penalties against her would constitute disability-based
discrimination under Title II of the ADA.
Legal Basis for Accommodation Request
Under Title II of the ADA (28 C.F.R. § 35.130(b)(7)), public entities must reasonably modify policies, practices, or
procedures to accommodate individuals with disabilities unless the city can demonstrate that the modification would
fundamentally alter the nature of the program or impose an undue burden.
City’s Violation if Denied:
Frequent displacement disrupts her ability to function, worsening her cognitive struggles and difficulty with 
problem-solving in an unstable environment.
Removing her tent eliminates her only stable and disability-adapted shelter, which directly impacts her 
ability to manage daily life tasks.
Issuing citations or arresting her for failing to relocate would penalize her for a disability-related limitation, 
violating ADA protections against discrimination.
The city has not provided an alternative ADA-compliant shelter, meaning forced relocation would place her life 
at severe risk without a suitable alternative available.
Request for Follow-Up & Next Steps

You have submitted substantially similar requests on behalf of numerous individuals over the past several
months, and you have previously been advised that such requests would constitute a fundamental
alteration of the City’s operations, impose an undue burden, or, in the case of shelter, do not constitute a
form of reasonable accommodation under the ADA, regardless of any particular individual’s disabilities.
Under Title II of the ADA, the City may deny requests based on fundamental alteration or undue burden,
because of the impact that a particular request would have on the City and its programs or services. As I
have previously informed you, those impacts to the City are the same, regardless of the individual’s
disability or the nexus for their request. The determination below was made by me in consultation with the
