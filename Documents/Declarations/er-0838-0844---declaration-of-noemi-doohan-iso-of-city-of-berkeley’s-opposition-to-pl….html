Rats are the primary vector to spread Leptospirosis so complete eradication of the rat population in this location is necessary to sufficiently mitigate risk of spread to humans, dogs, and other animals. 6. In my professional judgment, the conditions at the Harrison Corridor encampment materially elevate the risk of leptospirosis transmission to the Public because these environmental conditions support uncontrolled growth of rodent populations and related increase in potential contact between humans and rats, contaminated water, surfaces, or materials. 7. Eradication of the rats in the Harrison Corridor, as opposed to suppression or control, is necessary to control the spread of Leptospirosis because the bacteria is spread by rat body fluids, including urine, and is able to remain alive in the soil and any standing water for up to 30 days, and potentially longer depending on conditions. Any rats remaining in and around the encampment are likely to be continually exposed and potentially infected by this residual bacteria in the environment. Those remaining infected rats will then produce more contagion through their urine, starting the cycle of infection all over again. 8. In January 2026, following the December 2025 detection of Leptospirosis in rats and dogs, the City began extensive public health outreach at the Harrison Corridor encampment. This outreach included offering alternative shelter, providing information to encampment residents regarding leptospirosis and rodent-borne disease risks and advising on practices intended to reduce exposure risk. Coordinated in-person outreach efforts to communicate general information and provide resources (including but not limited to personal protective equipment, Leptospirosis canine vaccine vouchers, and hygiene materials) regarding Leptospirosis to the Harrison Street corridor began on January 13, 2026, and continued weekly. Through these weekly in-person events that started on January 13, 2026, City staff engaged in direct outreach and education efforts, including visits to the Harrison Street area, communication with local businesses and encampment residents, and coordination with partner agencies. The presence of Leptospirosis at or associated with the encampment affects the safety of City of Berkeley staff operations involving handling or disturbing materials that may constitute rodent harborage, including sorting, moving, or manipulating contaminated or potentially contaminated items. 10. Based on my public health responsibilities and professional judgment, reducing Leptospirosis risk requires a substantial reduction in rodent harborage and exposure pathways. 11. Complete clearance of the Harrison encampment is the most reliable way to eliminate harborage and Leptospirosis exposure risks at this specific site. 12. Where complete clearance is not feasible due to legal constraints, partial abatement measures can still reduce risk. 13. A partial abatement can reduce the risk of Leptospirosis by decreasing the number of individuals exposed to unsanitary conditions at the site and the volume of trash, food waste, longterm vehicles, and makeshift structures that provide rodent harborage and facilitate rodent persistence. 14. I have been in communication with Alameda County Public Health officials regarding the conditions at the Harrison Street encampment and the presence of leptospirosis risk factors in that area. This communication has included correspondence with Dr. Why of Alameda County Vector Control regarding rodent activity, environmental contamination, and appropriate public health responses. In those communications, Dr. Why and I discussed the challenges associated with conducting rodent abatement and environmental remediation in a densely occupied encampment environment. While full access to the entire affected area would provide the most effective opportunity to identify rodent burrows and address environmental conditions contributing to rodent activity, we also discussed that partial abatement measures can still provide meaningful public health benefits. In my professional judgment, partial abatement and remediation efforts are therefore worthwhile and can contribute to improving public health conditions even if they cannot immediately or substantially eliminate all sources of rodent activity and risk of Leptospirosis transmission. 15. I have personally visited and observed conditions at the Harrison Street encampment area in connection with my duties as the City of Berkeley’s Public Health Officer. During those site visits, I observed numerous stationary vehicles and recreational vehicles (“RVs”) that had remained in the same location for extended periods of time. In several instances, these vehicles were surrounded by accumulated materials, debris, and stored belongings that can provide food sources and shelter for rodents. Based on my observations and my experience addressing rodent-borne disease risks in urban environments, stationary vehicles can create conditions that allow rodents to harbor underneath or around them, particularly when the vehicles remain unmoved for long periods and when surrounding materials provide nesting opportunities and food sources. Areas beneath vehicles that remain in place for extended periods can also make it difficult for vector control personnel to inspect for burrows or place traps or kill rats with poisonous substances. For these reasons, stationary RVs and other vehicles that remain in the same location for extended periods can contribute to environmental conditions that support rodent activity and associated public health risks. Moving these vehicles allows City staff and vector control personnel to access previously obstructed ground surfaces and address rodent harborage conditions. 16. The Harrison Street encampment contains numerous structures, tents, and vehicles positioned in close proximity to one another, as well as combustible materials such as wood pallets, propane tanks, fuel containers, and other flammable debris. Based on information provided to me by City staff involved in encampment response and emergency services, these conditions significantly increase the risk of uncontrolled fires. Fires have already occurred at this encampment on multiple occasions, including most recently a fire in January 2026. 17. In addition to increasing the likelihood that a fire may start or spread rapidly, the current configuration of tents, vehicles, and accumulated belongings within the Harrison encampment impedes the ability of fire and emergency medical personnel to safely access individuals in the event of a fire or other emergency. Emergency response vehicles require sufficient clearance to enter and maneuver within an area. When access routes are obstructed or narrowed by structures, debris, or parked vehicles, response times are delayed and emergency personnel may be unable to safely reach individuals who require assistance. 18. From a public health and safety perspective, it is important that the City maintain adequate emergency access and reduce conditions that increase the likelihood or severity of fires which can cause additional Public Health risk. Removing structures and vehicles that obstruct emergency access routes is therefore an important component of protecting the safety of encampment residents, emergency responders, and the surrounding community. 19. Certain vehicles located within the Harrison encampment are being used as habitation units and have remained in the same location for extended periods of time. Vehicles that remain stationary for long durations in areas with documented rodent activity often function as sites of rodent harborage, particularly when food waste, stored belongings, or structural gaps allow rodents to nest within or beneath the vehicle. 20. In order to effectively address rodent harborage and environmental contamination, pest control and environmental health personnel must be able to access the underlying ground surfaces where rodents may burrow and travel. Vehicles that remain parked in the same location prevent personnel from accessing those areas to inspect for burrows, place traps, and conduct rodent abatement activities. Moving these vehicles allows vector control and environmental health teams to reach previously inaccessible areas and implement effective rodent control measures. 21. From a public health perspective, reducing environmental conditions that support uncontrolled rodent population growth is a critically important step in controlling the spread of leptospirosis and other rodent-borne diseases. 22. Plaintiffs assert that requiring individuals to leave the encampment will spread leptospirosis throughout the City. Uncontrolled growth of rat populations in an environment known to have had transmission of Leptospirosis from rats to dogs is the primary concern for spread of Leptospirosis in this area. Infected rats are the primary vector for the spread of leptospirosis in humans, dogs and other animals. People, their dogs, or their possessions are not the primary vector for the spread of leptospirosis. Relocating the property of encampment residents does not present the same level of public health risk as an uncontrolled rat population being allowed to continue to grow in the setting of leptospirosis. While there is risk in handling possessions that could be contaminated with leptospirosis, the city has had an ongoing program in place to dispose of those belongings at the encampment. Addressing the environmental conditions posed by rats in this encampment requires access to the affected area so that sanitation, rodent control, and environmental remediation measures can be implemented. 23. Allowing the encampment to remain in place would prevent the City and its public health and vector control personnel from accessing areas where rodent burrows and contamination are present and would allow those environmental conditions to persist. Temporarily relocating individuals and vehicles from the affected area allows the City to access the site, implement rodent abatement measures, and address the environmental conditions contributing to the presence and spread of leptospirosis. 24. As part of my role as the City of Berkeley’s Public Health Officer, I provide guidance to City departments regarding public health risks and appropriate safety practices for City employees. In connection with the leptospirosis concerns identified at the Harrison Street encampment area, I issued public health guidance regarding the handling of materials located within the affected area. That guidance advised City staff that they should not sort, pack, store, or manually handle belongings or materials from the Harrison Street encampment area.
