It is important to note that Leptospirosis is considered a “neglected tropical disease” which is prevalent in underdeveloped tropical countries and associated with insufficient sanitation and barriers to access health care.13 Leptospirosis is rare in developed countries. As such it is rarely seen by clinicians practicing in the US as this lethal disease is known to have very low prevalence in the US. The exception is homeless encampments in the US which are considered high risk for rat infestations and associated Lepto outbreaks in rats.14 The rats are a vector for contagion and can potentially spread the Lepto disease through their urine and body fluids and via unsanitary accumulations of water and mud to humans, dogs and other animals living in the encampment or passing nearby the areas around the encampment. The contagion can also be transmitted in bodies of water containing the urine and body fluids of infected animals. I recently inspected the encampment, looking for signs of risk for continued spread of Lepto in this area. I also discussed the conditions with the City of Berkeley Environmental Health Division. Conditions at the encampment include extensive, uncontained and long standing harborage of rats, presumably including in the encampment dwellings and vehicles themselves; standing water and mud in puddles throughout the encampment including around the dwellings which can spread the Lepto from infected animal urine and body fluids to humans; lack of containment of food which attracts and can feed rats and contribute to expansion of the rat vector for this contagion; lack of containment of dogs which might be asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic and infected with Lepto and spread the contagion; and an adjacent creek which may be used for bathing and spread the contagion given there is no clean running water at the encampment to bathe in regularly. Of note, on the opposite shore of the creek, there is a large University of California housing complex for families, and the creek itself has a park and bike path that runs along it with the possibility of wild animals (such as racoons), people and their pets being exposed to the water in the creek which could be contaminated with Leptospirosis. The spread of Lepto from infected animals would be through their urine or body fluids into water. As the rats and dogs are uncontained, there is risk to the public beyond the confines of the area of the encampment. Lepto can spread to other animals (including neighbors’ pets) that are exposed to infected urine containing water and encampment human residents as well as neighbors and passers-by if they are exposed to the infected urine. For example, if a person was walking through a puddle infected with Lepto and they have a cut on their foot and are wearing open-toed shoes, they could be potentially infected. The water in the creek has not yet been tested for the presence of Lepto; however, the presence of Lepto so close to the creek means that there is also a significant risk of the disease spreading to the creek and wildlife if the nearby infected rodent population is not controlled. 19. Lepto is found throughout the world, particularly among marginalized communities that do not have access to indoor and refrigerated food storage, running water, and indoor plumbing. This describes the conditions in the Harrison Corridor as well. 20. Temporary sanitation/hygiene services like port-a-potties and outdoor washing stations would not have prevented the current situation because they would not have prevented the prevalence of rats. Additionally, such services would be ineffective to address the current occurrence of Leptospirosis. 21. In order to remove the risk of further spread of rat borne Lepto, the entire encampment (tents, structures, vehicles) must be removed to allow for complete eradication of rodent burrows. a. Vehicles—especially RVs that stay in one place for a long time—are attractive rodent harborages, so cleaning around them is not sufficient. b. People should be instructed not to re-encamp within 1/3 mile because rats can travel and will travel to a new encampment site with similar unsanitary conditions including lack of proper food storage. Studies of Lepto spread via rat vectors support that rats can travel 1/3 of a mile from the epicenter of rat outbreaks.16 c. Cleanup will take at least 30 days because that is how long Lepto can live in the soil. Additionally, eradication of rats requires many cycles of baiting the rats with poison in their underground burrows, removing the carcasses and rebaiting over a period of time. 22. The City of Berkeley Local Public Health Jurisdiction (“LHJ”) intends to work with Alameda County LHJ and its community partners to contain this Lepto outbreak in rats, and possibly in dogs (which as of yet have not been tested other than those that were sick and dying), and to protect the public. The absence of confirmed human cases is reassuring but does not remove the risk of undiagnosed cases or future cases. If a human case were confirmed, that would elevate the urgency of the recommended response to protect human life and other animal life in the City of Berkeley. 24. City of Berkeley prevents the City of Berkeley from implementing fully effective eradication efforts for the Lepto rat outbreak at the Harrison Street encampment. As a result, the City is using alternative means to protect the health of the people and animals living in the encampment and those passing by. However, these alternatives cannot adequately address the risks to human health posed by the presence of Lepto in the Harrison Street encampment. Leptospirosis Surveillance Leptospirosis is the most widespread zoonosis in the world and is most common in temperate and tropical zones. It is caused by a bacterium that is spread through the urine of infected animals, which can get into water and soil where it can survive for weeks to months at a time. Various domestic and wild animals can carry the bacteria and excrete it for months to several years. Dogs are the most infected domestic animal. Humans can become infected through contact with urine, or other bodily fluids (except saliva) from infected animals or contact with soil, water or food that has been contaminated with the urine from infected animals. People most at risk are those that work outdoors or with animals. ER-1614

S Plague is a bacterial disease associated with certain rodents, other small mammals, and their fleas. It is famously known as the “black death” and was . : responsible for killing millions throughout Europe during the Middle Ages. The bacteria that cause this disease - Yersinia pestis - can currently be found in parts of Africa, Asia, and the Western United States. However, prior to its introduction this pathogen was not present in the US. It is believed that it was introduced a to the West Coast from rat-infested ships arriving to San Francisco from China in 1900. Within 2-3 years, over 100 known plague cases were reported in San Francisco and by 1909 plague was detected in Alameda County in rats, ground squirrels, and humans. By 1910, plague had spread to eight California counties. Over the decades surveillance and detection of plague in Alameda County has been sporadic and minimal. It was last found in 1981 in the eastern part of the < 4 County when a ground squirrel and two coyotes tested positive. In 2023, in = zd conjunction with the California Department of Public Health, District staff © . conducted plague surveillance in small mammals for the first time in decades.

’ In total, five ground squirrels and 20 Peromyscus mice were trapped and tested. “a All samples were negative for plague. There are plans to resume plague surveillance soon. West Nile Virus Surveillance Our District has been assisting with monitoring for the presence and spread of West Nile Virus in Alameda County for many years.
