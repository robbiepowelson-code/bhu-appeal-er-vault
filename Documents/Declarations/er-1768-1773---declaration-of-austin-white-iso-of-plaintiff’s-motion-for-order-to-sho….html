For the past two and a half years, I have lived at the encampment located at 8th and Harrison Streets. Before that, I stayed at the Golden Bear Inn, a permanent supportive housing site, until I was wrongfully evicted. Prior to that, I camped at People’s Park and along the I-80 freeway ramp near University Avenue. Every encampment where I have lived in Berkeley has been closed. 3. I live with multiple disabilities, including esophageal varices, a condition I have had since I was three years old. This condition makes it medically dangerous for me to lift objects over 20 pounds. I also have PTSD and other mental health conditions that affect my ability to manage stress, change and engage with authority figures. 4. On July 11, 2025, I attended an in-person meeting with the City of Berkeley’s ADA Coordinator and other city officials to discuss my request for reasonable accommodations under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA). I was accompanied by officers of the Berkeley Homeless Union, Yesica Prado and Gordon Gilmore, and officer Robbie Powelson of the California Homeless Union and LifeLong Medical Nurse Practitioner Olivia DeBree, who were there to support me at the meeting. 5. I entered the meeting in good faith, expecting a two-way dialogue about accommodations that would allow me to avoid harm and maintain access to all city programs provided at the encampment, which are essential resources to my survival. 6. Instead, I was met with City staff reading statements from their computers, denying my requests without considering my specific disabilities and deflecting questions. Anything that was proposed was just me with a no. I asked questions, but none were answered. I did not feel heard. At the meeting, I shared the extreme physical and emotional stress I live under every day just trying to get food, water, safety and protect my belongings and support animals. I also questioned why the City keeps destroying my property, even though I try to keep my area clean. Everything I own has been taken three times in less than a year. 8. I became very emotional while talking about what happened during the June 4 sweep. I was woken up around 6:30 a.m. by a group of police officers using nonlethal weapons. They gave me just 20 minutes to pack and leave my campsite. I had a foot injury from a car accident at the time and my esophageal condition makes it dangerous for me to lift heavy items. Still, I was violently arrested, slammed to the ground against my skateboard by a dozen police officers who put their knees on me. I lost everything and was jailed at Santa Rita for five days. I’m still facing charges for this day. While telling this story in the meeting, I started crying and had to step outside to decompress. It made me feel distraught and upset that we were not there to discuss solutions. Contingent ADA talks do not solve the problem. 9. The City staff did not ask me how I wanted to be addressed. The City Attorney Iris Mattes said, “whatever you like to be called” in a dismissive tone. Throughout the meeting, they focused on getting me to comply with leaving the encampment to an unknown location, not on my safety or what I need to survive. I made several requests during the meeting including: I asked to be allowed to stay where I am temporarily, until the City can identify a stable, safe place for me to go. I said, “Where I am right now: I’m safe and I feel comfortable.” I keep my camping area clean and respectable. 11. I asked where I’m supposed to go if this abatement happens. I said, “If this abatement happens, then where am I going to go? To jail? Again?” I’ve been homeless over 30 years and still have no idea where I can exist without being punished. I often question: where do I go in the city that I call home? There’s nowhere left to go. 12. I asked to keep all of my essential property, including my house and supplies for my emotional support animals. My tent is my home and critical to my health and survival. I don’t have a door I can keep locked, but I can zip up for my personal safety. 13. I asked to keep access to services at my current location, including bathrooms, trash pickup and outreach workers. I rely on County medical services and housing navigation that come to this encampment. If I’m moved, I’ll lose that support. I would have to start over with nothing once again. It has happened so much, I am starting to feel defeated to keep trying and living. 14. The City did not offer me any alternatives. I asked directly to Thomas Gregory, “Where am I supposed to go? What am I supposed to do? What’s your suggestions?” No one answered. One of the City Attorneys finally said, “Mr. White, unfortunately I have to repeat what we’ve continued to say is we’re not in a position to answer that question.” 15. I felt helpless and angry, so I left the building to return to my dogs. The meeting felt like it was set up to deny me, not help me. That made me break down. 16. Several things stood out to me during the meeting: Mr. Gregory admitted that sweeps are harmful. He said, “I am aware that abetments are difficult on campers and traumatic.” I believe I could have died on the latest sweep of June 4, from the brutality I faced that aggravated my disability. Yet, the City continued the meeting without offering any safer alternatives. 17. Mr. Gregory admitted there are no ADA-compliant shelter beds available. They also said there are no non-congregate beds right now, just congregate shelters that only operate overnight. I cannot go into those environments because of my medical and mental health conditions. I don’t feel safe sleeping there. 18. On July 25, HRT staff member Epsilon Galloway came to the camp and told me he wouldn’t help with housing services because “he’s already been helped twice.” I felt written off and discarded. Furthermore, city staff continue to frequent the encampment despite the union attorney’s cease and desist order to seize their operations. 19. The City’s abatement policies and sidewalk laws do not take into account how my disabilities work. They expect me to lift, pack, and move everything I own with very little notice of 24 hours, something I physically cannot do without risking a medical emergency. The nine-square-foot limit makes it impossible for me to sleep safely with my gear and my dogs. And when they take my property, they don’t treat it as medically necessary, city staff just throw it away. I’ve lost clothes, shoes, food, medication, bike, therapeutic art supplies, dog food, blankets, bed, musical instruments, tent — my whole livelihood.
