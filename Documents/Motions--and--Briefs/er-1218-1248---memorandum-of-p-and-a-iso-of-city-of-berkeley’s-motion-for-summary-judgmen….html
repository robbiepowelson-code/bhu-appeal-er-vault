INTRODUCTION Defendants bring this summary judgment motion pursuant to the Court’s recent order setting an expedited briefing schedule. ECF 202. The City objects to the Court’s decision to compel the City to move for summary judgment on two-weeks’ notice, delineate the precise points that must be addressed, and set an expedited briefing schedule when discovery has not closed and the dispositive motion deadline is not for several months. Without waiving these objections, the City brings this motion as ordered and is entitled to summary judgment on all of Berkeley Homeless Union (“BHU”)’s claims. First, BHU lacks standing. At the summary judgment stage it can no longer rely on mere allegations, and it has not met its burden to identify, for each form of relief a specific member with standing for its ADA claims, nor has it offered concrete evidence to support its standing allegations for the constitutional claims. Second, regarding BHU’s ADA claims, the City engaged in good faith interactive process discussions with BHU for almost a year and, as a result, granted reasonable accommodations, offered alternatives where feasible, and explained when requests were not required under Title II of the ADA. As such, there is no dispute of material fact that the City complied with the ADA. Third, as the Court already recognized at the preliminary injunction stage, BHU cannot support its claim that the City subjects its members to state created danger. The City has not placed campers in any danger that is not inherent to being unhoused, and BHU’s relied-upon harm is either purely hypothetical or factually incorrect. Lastly, BHU has not established that the City has seized its property in violation of the Fourth Amendment. There is no dispute that the City provided more than the required notice and any property seized was stored or discarded in accordance with established law. II. LEGAL STANDARD Summary judgment is proper “if the pleadings, depositions, answers to interrogatories, and admissions on file, together with the affidavits, if any, show that there is no genuine issue as to any material fact and that the moving party is entitled to a judgment as a matter of law.” Celotex Corp. v. Catrett, 477 U.S. 317, 317 (1986); see also Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. The moving party “bears the initial” burden of demonstrating “the absence of a genuine issue of material fact.” Celotex Corp., 477 U.S. at 323. But the moving party need not negate the opponent’s claims. Ibid. In fact, “the burden on the moving party may be discharged by ‘showing’…that there is an absence of evidence to support the nonmoving party’s case.” Id. at 325. The Court’s Order Requiring Summary Judgment Briefing is Improper As an initial matter, the City objects to the Court’s order requiring an expedited motion for summary judgment. First, the litigant, not the Court, is vested with the decision of whether to move for summary judgment. Neither the F.R.C.P. nor any caselaw requires that the City move for summary judgment as a whole or on any issue. The Court’s sua sponte determination ruling that the City must move for summary judgment on particular topics, when no party requested such briefing, effectively usurps the City’s authority to represent its own interests. Such judicial intervention is not within courts’ power. See United States v. Samuels, 808 F.2d 1298, 1301 (8th Cir. 1987) (“[C]ourts are essentially passive instruments of government… . We wait for cases to come to us, and when they do we normally decide only questions presented by the parties.”); Greenlaw v. United States, 554 U.S. 237, 244 (2008) (“[O]ur adversary system is designed around the premise that the parties know what is best for them, and are responsible for advancing the facts and arguments entitling them to relief.”) (internal modifications omitted). Second, ordering expedited briefing as part of a last-minute modification of the injunction, when the dispositive-motion deadline is months away, is especially problematic. As explained more fully in the City’s motion to dissolve (ECF No. 207), a court can only modify an injunction if a party moves for it. See Fed. R. Civ. P. 65(a)(1); see also Sequen v. Albarran, No. 25-CV-06487-PCP, 2025 WL 3773755, at *2 (N.D. Cal. Dec. 31, 2025) (court will not substantively modify a preliminary injunction at a status conference; “only pursuant to a properly filed motion.”). Modifying the injunction without such a motion was improper. Third, the Court’s order has forced the City to move for summary judgment before the Court rules on the City’s motion to dismiss. Although that motion was filed over seven months ago and argued in part that BHU does not have standing, the Court never ruled on the motion or even held a hearing on it. ECF 107. Because the City moved on standing grounds, the Court had an obligation to assure itself of BHU’s entitlement to this forum before allowing the litigation to proceed. See Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Env’t Servs. (TOC), Inc., 528 U.S. 167, 191 (2000) (“[I]f a plaintiff lacks standing at the time the action commences, [he is not entitled to] a federal judicial forum.”). B. BHU Lacks Standing to Bring This Suit BHU must establish associational standing to sue on behalf of its members.2 Satanic Temple v. Labrador, 149 F.4th 1047, 1050-54 (9th Cir. 2025). Under the well-established Hunt test, a plaintiff has associational standing only when: (1) its members otherwise would have standing to sue in their own right, (2) the interests it seeks to protect are germane to the entity’s purpose, and (3) neither the claim asserted nor the relief requested requires the participation of the individual members in the suit. Assoc. Gen. Contractors of Am., S.D. Chapter, Inc. v. Cal. Dep’t of Transp., 713 F.3d 1187, 1194 (9th Cir. 2013). Importantly, “standing is not dispensed in gross.” Lewis v. Casey, 518 U.S. 343, 358 n.6, (1996). “Just as an individual must demonstrate standing for each claim he seeks to press” and ‘for each form of relief sought,’ so too must an association that relies upon an individual member for standing purposes.” Waskul v. Washtenaw Cnty. Community Mental Health, 900 F.3d 250, 253 (6th Cir. 2018). As discussed below, BHU lacks standing for any of its claims. 2 BHU conceded that it does not intend to assert organizational standing. ECF No. 109, Tr. 5:9-23. In any event, BHU lacks organizational standing because it has put forth no facts showing that the City’s actions have interfered with its “core business activities.” See Food & Drug Admin. v. All. for Hippocratic Med., 602 U.S. 367, 394 (2024). A mere setback to its “abstract social interests” is not enough. Id. City of Ridgefield, 439 F.3d 1055, 1062 (9th Cir. 2006). BHU has not established any of these prongs. As to the first prong, BHU’s declarations provide no concrete details or evidence of particularized danger caused by the City. ECF Nos. 1, 72- 2, 123, 125, 200. For example, one BHU member asserted that City staff have forced him to move from numerous locations due to encampment closures, but he identifies no specific danger that arose as result. ECF No. 123, pg. 7. Similarly, another BHU member described his experience with numerous evictions but details no particularized danger. ECF No. 125. Simply put, there is no evidence on the record that any BHU member has been injured or exposed to any level of danger by the City. As for the second prong, the alleged injuries are also not foreseeable; indeed, they are in many instances factually incorrect and, in others, merely hypothetical. BHU provides a laundry list of potential dangers including lack of access to public restrooms, services offered by the Dorothy Day House, or medical care, and losing the support of their community. ECF Nos. 104 and 82. But as the City has explained, other restrooms remain available to campers, there is a large distance between the current encampment and Dorothy Day House making it infeasible for campers’ regular use, and the City’s medical and support services operate as a mobile unit and are equally available regardless of one’s location within the City. ECF No. 82. The Court previously found that BHU’s arguments were not persuasive at the preliminary injunction stage and, considering no further evidence has been introduced to support BHU’s claims of foreseeable injury, BHU cannot prevail at summary judgment. Finally, BHU also fails to meet the third prong of the state-created danger test because a city’s alleged lack of social services does not amount to the City acting with deliberate indifference. While the “general circumstances of being homeless [] cannot be minimized,” courts have explained that there can be no viable state-created danger claim “without allegations of intentional eviction during precarious weather or other facts indicating deliberate indifference to the safety and welfare of the population.” Cobine v. City of Eureka, 250 F. Supp. 3d 423, 433 (N.D. Cal. 2017); see also Reed v. City of Emeryville, 568 F. Supp. 3d 1029, 1040 (N.D. Cal. 2021); see also Young v. City of Los Angeles, No. CV2000709JFWRAO, 2020 WL 616363, at *7 (C.D. Cal. Feb. 10, 2020). (“Additionally, Plaintiff’s general complaints that the City is not doing enough to assist his social needs [] are not sufficient to state a substantive due process claim as there is no affirmative right to governmental aid.”) For instance, in Cobine, Plaintiffs alleged that the closure of their encampment community forced them to move to “unfamiliar areas”, “without the support of the community”, and made them “vulnerable to assault, theft, harassment, and worse.” Cobine, 250 F. Supp. at 433. Although the Court accepted that the encampment closure disbanded plaintiffs’ community environment, it dismissed their state-created danger claim because it sought relief only for the “generalized dangers of living on the street” which “preexisted Plaintiffs’ relocation,” and did not amount to “a deliberate indifference of placing Plaintiffs in an inherently more dangerous situation than they had faced previously.” Id. Similarly, in Reed, while Plaintiffs alleged that the congregate shelter might exacerbate their mental health, such a claim did not mean that the City placed plaintiffs in a more dangerous situation than they would be in while living in an encampment next to an active construction site. Reed v. City of Emeryville, 568 F. Supp. 3d 1029, 1040 (N.D. Cal. 2021). In this case, Plaintiffs have not introduced any evidence to the record that closing the encampment amounts to more than the “generalized danger of living on the street” that requires movement to unfamiliar areas, which alone is insufficient to show that the City acted with deliberate indifference. Accordingly, BHU has not established and cannot establish that the City has subjected its members to a state created danger. In fact, the Supreme Court recently held that ordinances which prohibit sleeping or camping on public property do not amount to a constitutional violation. See City of Grants Pass v. Johnson, 603 U.S. 520, 560-561 (2024). And the California Governor has encouraged municipalities to disband encampments with only forty-eight hours’ notice. See Executive Order N-1-24. Because government employees would have no notice that taking steps to close an encampment would amount to a constitutional violation, all individual defendants are entitled to qualified immunity on the statecreated danger and property seizure claims as a matter of law.
