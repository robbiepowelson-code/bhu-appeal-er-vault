CITY OF BERKELEY, Defendants. Case No. 23-cv-04537-EMC ORDER GRANTING IN PART AND DENYING IN PART MOTION FOR TEMPORARY RESTRAINING ORDER Docket No. 178 I. INTRODUCTION Before the Court is Plaintiffs’ Motion for Temporary Restraining Order (“TRO”). TRO Motion (Dkt. 178). The TRO motion concerns the City of Berkeley’s planned abatement of the 8th and Harrison encampment. Plaintiffs ask this Court to order the City to: (1) designate safe relocation sites for residents to relocate to without further risk of abatement, (2) assist residents with vehicles in relocating their vehicles, and (3) provide residents with replacements for any property left behind and destroyed after the abatement. Id. The City justifies the planned abatement as a necessary public health intervention to address the growing leptospirosis outbreak in and around the encampment. Opposition to TRO (Dkt. 185). The City emphasizes that all individuals protected by the existing preliminary injunction in the related case Berkeley Homeless Union v. City of Berkeley, No. 3:25-cv-01414- EMC, are expressly exempt from the City’s abatement order. Id.; see also Emily Roznowski Declaration (Dkt. 179), Ex. B. For the reasons discussed below, the TRO motion is GRANTED IN PART AND DENIED IN PART. LEGAL STANDARD The standard for issuing a TRO is substantially identical to the standard for issuing a preliminary injunction. Washington v. Trump, 847 F.3d 1151, 1159 n.3 (9th Cir. 2017). A party seeking a TRO must establish “[1] that he is likely to succeed on the merits, [2] that he is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief, [3] that the balance of equities tips in his favor, and [4] that an injunction is in the public interest.” Winter v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 555 U.S. 7, 20 (2008). An injunction is a matter of equitable discretion and is “an extraordinary remedy that may only be awarded upon a clear showing that the plaintiff is entitled to such relief.” Winter, 555 U.S. at 22. III. DISCUSSION The Court finds that both the irreparable harm and balance of equities factors weigh in Plaintiffs’ favor. Encampment residents face the imminent loss of shelter, essential personal property, and community support networks. Berkeley does posit countervailing health concerns pertaining to the documented Leptospirosis outbreak within the encampment community as found in rats and animals, risking infection of humans. Plaintiffs debate the medical necessity of removing the entire encampment as opposed to other mitigation measures. The City counters with testimony from health professional advising removal the encampment to eradicate the disease. Even if the Court assumes the balance of hardships tips sharply in favor of Plaintiffs, for the reasons stated below, Plaintiffs have failed to raise serious questions on the merits with respect to most of its claims for relief. The Court turns to the specific relief sought and the likelihood of success on the merits. A. Designation of Relocation Site Plaintiffs request an order requiring the City to designate a safe relocation site where residents will not face further abatement actions. The Court cannot grant this relief. Plaintiffs have not identified any authority requiring a municipality to affirmatively designate a sanctioned encampment site as a precondition for abatement. The Fourth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments do not confer this right on Plaintiffs. While the Court previously recognized the value of community among unhoused populations in Boyd v. The Court also notes the City’s representation on the record that it does not maintain a city-wide blanket ban on sleeping outdoors, and that certain conduct and space-restriction regulations, such as the 3x3 space occupation rule, which might make camping on the streets and sidewalks impracticable are not administratively enforced per the City Council absent certain defined circumstances. For the foregoing reasons, Plaintiffs have not shown a likelihood of success in asserting this relief. Accordingly, the TRO motion is DENIED IN PART with respect to this requested relief. B. Replacement of Destroyed Property Plaintiffs seek an order requiring the City to provide replacement survival equipment, such as tents, sleeping bags, mobility aids, for any property that the City intends to destroy during the abatement. The Court denies this relief as well. The Fourth and Fourteenth Amendments require the City to provide adequate notice and a meaningful opportunity to retrieve unabandoned personal property before it is seized or destroyed. Lavan v. City of L.A., 693 F.3d 1022, 1032–33 (9th Cir. 2012). Here, the City has provided advance notice (effectively weeks) and an opportunity to remove property. This satisfied due process. See id.; Shipp v. Schaaf, 379 F. Supp. 3d 1033, 1038 (N.D. Cal. 2019). And the City retains authority to destroy abandoned, potentially contaminated property without compensation for public health purposes. Miller v. Schoene, 276 U.S. 272, 279–80 (1928); Pena v. City of L.A., 158 F.4th 1033, 1037–38 (9th Cir. 2025). The Constitution does not require the City to furnish replacements for abandoned property destroyed during an abatement which accords due process, particularly an abatement predicated on public health grounds. While the Court notes the tension in the City’s position — declaring property too hazardous for its employees to handle or store while directing residents to move that same property elsewhere — this inconsistency does not give rise to a constitutional obligation to supply replacement property. C. Seizure of Vehicles This Court has previously held that, under the Fourth Amendment, the warrantless seizure of a vehicle requires a fact-specific determination that the vehicle presents an obstruction to traffic or a specific threat to public safety; a generalized showing is insufficient. Prado v. City of Berkeley, No. 23-cv-04537-EMC, Motion to Dismiss (Dkt. 87) at 26–27 (“The City has the burden to establish that the taking was warranted on a fact-specific basis, … Moreover, the City has the burden to prove that the vehicles they impounded were ‘impeding traffic or threatening public safety and convenience.’”). The City has not met that requirement. The City has merely offered a general assertion that stationary vehicles may harbor rodent nests or impede abatement efforts. Opposition to TRO, Ex. 1 (Peter Radu Declaration) at ¶¶ 18–21. There have been no individualized inspections or evaluations of any vehicles in this regard. Moreover, the City’s claim that it is impossible to search for signs of rat infestation under the vehicles makes little sense, especially considering the city could assist residents in moving the vehicle several feet to obtain access to the ground. As this Court previously held, a vehicle posing no specific, documented threat to public safety or traffic may not be seized under the community caretaker doctrine. Order Granting in Part and Denying in Part Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss (Dkt 51) at 23–27. The TRO is therefore GRANTED with respect to vehicle seizure. The City may not seize, impound or destroy any vehicle in the planned abatement zone absent a specific, fact-based determination that the particular vehicle obstructs traffic or poses a concrete public safety threat.
