1 INTRODUCTION Homelessness is “a complex and difficult human issue.” City of Grants Pass, Or. v. Johnson, 603 U.S. 520, 535 (2024). Addressing it requires “the full panoply of tools in the policy toolbox” to balance helping unhoused residents with protecting community health and safety. Id. For this reason, the people and their elected representatives, not federal judges, are best positioned to devise an effective response. Id. The district court here, however, has repeatedly substituted its own judgment for the people’s. For more than year, it blocked a critical health and safety encampment abatement despite a deadly Leptospirosis outbreak. In doing so, it routinely disregarded basic civil procedure principles and binding precedent. First, in orders already on appeal, the court granted Berkeley Homeless Union (“BHU”) what became a nine-month preliminary injunction on a ground— the City’s supposed failure to “complete” the Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”) interactive process—that the court later recognized had no legal basis. 10-ER-1864. With little analysis, the court also erroneously found that BHU, which purports to be an association but has never produced evidence proving it, had standing to pursue ADA reasonable accommodations claims related to the abatement for an unknown number of unhoused individuals with different disabilities and needs. 10-ER-1863-65. SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT First, the district court erred in finding BHU had associational standing for ADA reasonable accommodations and state-danger claims. Such claims are too fact-specific to support associational standing. See, e.g., NetChoice, LLC, 152 F.4th at 1014. Although the court ignored this third element of the associational standing test, it could not do so, as the requirement is not optional. BHU also lacks standing for all its claims for other reasons, including that it failed to provide admissible evidence, and that associations lack standing to assert Fourth Amendment claims vicariously. See Knife Rights, Inc. v. Vance, 802 F.3d 377, 387 (2d Cir. 2015). Second, the court erred in ruling in Plaintiffs’ favor on their Fourth Amendment claims challenging the possible impoundment, despite ample notice, of vehicles used as shelter. Binding precedent, which the court ignored, permits cities to impound vehicles, even those used as shelter, for valid public health and safety reasons like the ones here. See, e.g., Opperman, 428 U.S. at 368-69. Third, the court erred in ruling for Plaintiffs on the state-created danger doctrine. The doctrine only applies when the state causes serious bodily harm such Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (32 of 74)

p. 33

24 as assault, sexual abuse, or death, which the plaintiff would not have faced otherwise. 3PAK LLC, 174 F.4th at 1184-85. The only harms from abatement are inherent to homelessness, which the City did not cause. And even if the abatement were to slightly worsen Plaintiffs’ situation, any resulting harm does not rise to the high level required to support a claim. Id. BHU Lacks Standing for ADA and State-Created Danger Claims Because They Require Individualized Proof. Organizations like BHU must establish associational standing or organizational standing. Satanic Temple v. Labrador, 149 F.4th 1047, 1051-54 (9th Cir. 2025). Only associational standing is at issue here. 10-ER-1806:19-22. BHU would have associational standing only if it could establish that (1) its members would have standing to sue in their own right; (2) the interests it seeks to protect are germane to its purpose; and (3) neither the claim asserted nor the relief requested requires the participation of individual members in the lawsuit. Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (34 of 74)

p. 35

26 Associated Gen. Contractors of Am., S.D. Chapter, Inc. v. Cal. Dep’t of Transp., 713 F.3d 1187, 1194 (9th Cir. 2013). Although BHU established none of these prongs, standing for its ADA and state-created danger claims fails most clearly on the third. Under that prong, associations lack standing for fact-specific claims requiring individualized proof because such claims are unmanageable without members’ participation in the lawsuit. NetChoice, LLC v. Bonta, 152 F.4th 1002, 1014 (9th Cir. 2025). “A reasonable accommodation claim, by its very nature, is fact-specific.” Bone v. Univ. of N. Carolina Health Care Sys., 678 F. Supp. 3d 660, 700-01 (M.D.N.C. 2023) (citing Wong v. Regents of Univ. of Cal., 192 F.3d 807, 818 (9th Cir. 1999)). It turns on each member’s disability and needs, requiring “participation and cooperation” by numerous individuals to ensure “successful judicial resolution.” Parent/Pro. Advoc. League v. Springfield, Mass., 934 F.3d 13, 34-35 (1st Cir. 2019). The record here shows as much. The court granted accommodations for some people, but not others, based on their individual circumstances, even though many of these individuals did not participate directly in the case. 1-ER-97-170. These claims have not been manageable—at the same time the court issued summary judgment for seven BHU members, it issued a “preliminary” injunction for sixteen more who did not begin participating until later, and it is not clear that Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (35 of 74)

The court’s need to bend the rules of civil procedure to manage BHU’s ADA claims shows precisely why it lacks standing for them as a matter of law. See Parent/Pro. Advoc. League, 934 F.3d at 34-35 (affirming dismissal of ADA reasonable accommodations claim for this reason). The same is true for BHU’s state-created danger claims. As the district court’s recent class certification ruling in Prado recognized, those claims are “plaintiff-specific” because they require assessing each person’s condition before and after the City’s action to determine whether the City placed each person in a more dangerous position. 2-ER-212-13. For example, the analysis may differ depending on the weather conditions the day each person is displaced. Id. Associations lack standing for such highly fact-specific claims. It does not matter that BHU only seeks injunctive relief. BHU cannot “automatically satisf[y] the third prong … simply by requesting equitable relief instead of damages.” Bano v. Union Carbide Corp., 361 F.3d 696, 714-15 (2d Cir. 2004). Injunctive relief claims are generally less likely to require individualized proof because liability and relief often turn on a question common to all association members. See, e.g., Nat’l Fed’n of the Blind of Cal. v. Uber Techs., Inc., 103 F. Supp. 3d 1073, 1079 (N.D. Cal. 2015) (injunctive relief claim challenging Uber’s policy against transporting guide dogs). By describing the third prong as “prudential,” the Supreme Court meant not that it is optional, but rather that Congress may override it by statute. That is, Congress may authorize associations to sue under certain statutes even though their members’ claims would otherwise be too fact-specific for associational standing. See United Food & Com. Workers Union Loc. 751 v. Brown Grp., Inc., 517 U.S. 544, 548-49 (1996). Congress has not done so here. Similarly, although the City does not enforce every violation of rules prohibiting long-term street parking, that does not create a “violate-the-parkinglaws” program. Simply put, the City is entitled to focus limited resources on the most dangerous conditions without inadvertently creating new programs it must then evaluate for modification. 6-ER-1295-1305. The District Court Erred in Ordering Accommodations That Fundamentally Alter the City’s Programs. The ADA does not require modifications that “fundamentally alter” public programs or impose “an undue financial or administrative burden” on public entities. Caltrans, 32 F.4th at 862 (citing Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509, 532 (2004)). The distinction between a reasonable modification and a fundamental alteration depends on how it impacts a program’s purpose. Id. Where, as here, a program is “designed to address risks to the public,” reasonableness depends on “the nature of the risk, whether the proposed modification would affect the Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (61 of 74)

53 agency’s ability to address the risk, and the probability of worsening the risk if the agency is forced to alter its programs.” Id. Here, the district court ruled in BHU’s favor on their requests to modify the City’s: (1) vehicle removal and abatement policies; (2) rule requiring sidewalk personal property be limited to occupying 9 square feet; and (3) storage and property management policies to require moving assistance and replacement of certain contaminated items. 1-ER-81-177. The district court’s rejection of the City’s fundamental alteration defense is erroneous. Aside from the issues with the court’s program definition and its use of the irrelevant fact that “not every violation triggers enforcement,” the court’s primary rationale was that the City failed to show that an exception for Ms. Dominguez or a “handful” of others would “substantially obstruct the City’s management of the program” or would “meaningfully disrupt parking turnover or emergency access.” 1-ER-144-45. As an initial matter, this analysis is dubious because the accommodation was not just for one person or a “handful” of people. Immediately after extending relief to Ms. Dominguez, the court granted the same relief to approximately 16 new alleged BHU members and then to the Prado plaintiffs with no further analysis. 1-ER-81-96,1-ER-171-77. But the court was also wrong as a legal matter to focus solely on the accommodation’s impact for one or a “handful” of individuals. The Supreme Court has explained that “fundamental alterations” occur “in two different ways.” PGA Tour, Inc. v. Martin, 532 U.S. 661, 682-83 (2001). First, if a modification request alters “an essential aspect” of the program, it is a fundamental alteration as a matter Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (63 of 74)

55 of law. Id. Second, if it alters a “less significant,” “peripheral” aspect of a program, then courts assess whether granting the accommodation to the individual in question would fundamentally alter the program. Id. In PGA Tour, it was “readily apparent that walking [was] not essential to the generalized game of golf,” so the court found that giving Martin, individually, a golf cart would not fundamentally alter the game. Martin v. PGA Tour, Inc., 204 F.3d 994, 999 (9th Cir. 2000). Here, however, the requested exemption alters an essential aspect (indeed, defeats the whole purpose) of the abatement action and parking restriction—to clear vehicles from public streets to preserve public health and shared use of street space. BHU’s requested exemption could only be “peripheral” to the City’s program if the City had a program allowing drivers to live in their vehicles on public streets. The City’s ban on long-term parking shows that it does not. The district court was also wrong to insist that the City satisfy a heightened evidentiary burden for its fundamental alteration defense. The City did submit extensive evidence, including a site visit by two departments that revealed serious problems with the vehicles in question. 3-ER-616-17 ¶¶ 5-7; 1-ER-619-35. But it did not need to. When an accommodation request would transform a program into something else entirely, it is a fundamental alteration even without any evidence. Shavelson v. Bonta, 608 F. Supp. 3d 919, 927 (N.D. Cal. 2022), is instructive. There, the court held that the plaintiffs’ requested accommodation from Case: 26-2755, 07/27/2026, DktEntry: 29.1, (64 of 74)

Id. The court thus held that because the requested accommodation would not just make the program less effective, but would “transform the benefit under the Act into something else entirely,” judgment was appropriate even without any discovery. Id. at 928-29. The same is true here. By requesting an exemption from City laws designed to protect broader public interests based on complex policy tradeoffs, BHU seeks to “transform the benefit” of those laws into something else entirely. ii.
